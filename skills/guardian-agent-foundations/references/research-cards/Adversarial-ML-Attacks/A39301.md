# A39301 On Robustness of Linear Classifiers to Targeted Data Poisoning

> CORPUS-LABEL NOTE: This paper is filed under the corpus category `Adversarial-ML-Attacks`, but its true subject is a **robustness-measurement / analysis tool** for defenders. It does not propose a new attack; it studies how to *quantify* a dataset's susceptibility to a targeted label-flipping poisoning attack against linear classifiers, and proves the measurement problem is NP-Complete. It is attack-adjacent (the metric it computes is "how few label flips does the attacker need"), so the label is defensible but should be read as "poisoning-robustness measurement," not "novel attack technique."

## Research question
Can a dataset's robustness against a **targeted, label-only data-poisoning attack** be automatically quantified for **linear classifiers**, under a **black-box victim** threat model — and can it be computed efficiently at scale despite the underlying combinatorial hardness? The paper defines robustness as the *minimum number of training-label perturbations* an adversary needs to force a chosen test point to be classified as a desired (wrong) class by some classifier in the victim's hypothesis space.

## Problem definition
- Task setting: binary classification from d-dimensional real features X ⊆ R^d to labels Y = {+1, −1}; classifier f_{w,b}(x) = sign(w^T x + b), w ∈ R^d, b ∈ R.
- Robustness r of a clean training set D_c = {(x_i, y_i) | i ∈ [m]} with respect to a target test point (x_t, y_t): the minimum number of training-label flips such that there exists a linear classifier f_{w,b} that (a) still fits the (poisoned) training labels and (b) classifies the target as the adversary's desired label y_t (where y_t is NOT the true class of x_t in D_c).
- Formally (paper's definition): r = min over the set 𝔓 of all possible poisoned datasets of Σ_{i=1}^m 𝕀(y'_i ≠ y_i), where a poisoned dataset must admit a classifier satisfying both fit-the-poisoned-labels and hit-the-target conditions.
- Interpretation: low robustness ⇒ few label flips suffice ⇒ dataset is easy to poison for that test point. The metric is also proposed as a user-facing tool: to identify the minimal set of training labels that could have influenced an undesirable model decision (contestability / audit use case).

## System or model being studied
- Victim models: **linear binary classifiers** only (SVM-like, logistic-regression-like — any sign(w^T x + b) hypothesis). The paper argues linear models remain relevant (perform well on many tasks; several recent works target them) and that this yields a *weaker/more realistic* threat model than attacks needing full model knowledge.
- Prototype tool: **ROBUSTRANGE** (Python). Computes both a lower bound ř and an upper bound r̂ on robustness r with the guarantee ř ≤ r ≤ r̂.
- Implementation: SCIP MILP solver (Bolusani et al. 2024) invoked within Google OR-Tools v9.12 for the lower bound; scikit-learn `SGDClassifier` (Pedregosa et al. 2011, v1.3.2) to learn classifiers from the augmented dataset for the upper bound.

## Threat model
- Attack class: **causative targeted attack** (Barreno et al. 2010) — a training-time (causative) attack on a specific test point.
- Adversary objective: make the victim's learned classifier assign a chosen target test point (x_t, y_t) the adversary's desired label y_t, while minimally perturbing the training set (targeted, not indiscriminate). Motivating example: a loan applicant who poisons training data so the trained model approves their loan.
- Adversary capability / budget: can **only flip labels** of a subset of clean training points (D_c → D_p, y'_i = y_i or y'_i = −y_i). Cannot modify features, cannot add/remove points. Budget = number of label flips (this is exactly the quantity being minimized/measured).
- Adversary knowledge: **knows the victim's hypothesis space** (that it is a linear classifier) but does **NOT** know the victim's training procedure, loss function, or the model's weights. The victim's training is treated as a **black box**. Rationale given: labels are easy to manipulate because label errors are common, especially when labels come from external sources (Adebayo et al. 2023); feature manipulation is harder.
- Box: **black-box victim** (from the attacker's perspective) — gray/white-box on the hypothesis class, black-box on procedure/weights.
- Timing: **training-time / causative** (poisoning). Target scope: **targeted** (single fixed test point; trigger-less — not backdoor/trigger-pattern based). Domain: **digital** (label-space perturbation). Adaptivity: the metric is defined over *any* classifier in the hypothesis space that satisfies the constraints, so it is not tied to a specific model instance — it is a worst-case-over-linear-classifiers quantity, not an adaptive attack on one model.
- Note: the paper's stated threat model is explicitly *weaker/more constrained* than prior work that assumes feature manipulation, add/modify/remove of points, or knowledge of the victim's learning process.

## Trust assumptions
- Training data provenance is untrusted at the label level (labels may come from external/crowd sources and be manipulated).
- Feature values are assumed trustworthy / non-manipulable (attack is label-only).
- The victim's training pipeline (loss, optimizer, weights) is opaque to the attacker; the paper's robustness metric deliberately quantifies over all linear classifiers so it does not need to trust knowledge of the exact victim training procedure.
- The defender/user running ROBUSTRANGE is assumed to have access to the clean training set D_c and the test point of interest.

## Attack or failure mechanism
- Mechanism: **targeted label-flipping poisoning**. The attacker flips the labels of a minimal subset S of training points so that a linear classifier fitting the poisoned labels is forced to place its decision boundary such that the target test point x_t lands on the desired (incorrect) side.
- Failure surface: because the hypothesis space is fixed but the training labels are corruptible, a small number of well-chosen flips can shift the feasible set of consistent linear separators to include one that misclassifies the target.
- Hardness result (why measuring this is hard): **Theorem 1** — given a dataset and a target test point, deciding whether its robustness r is less than a threshold κ is **NP-Complete** when the hypothesis class is linear binary classifiers. Membership in NP is shown via a witness classifier checkable by Gaussian elimination; NP-hardness is by reduction from **Vertex Cover** (Karp 2009): vertices/edges of a graph are encoded as training points, and a vertex cover of size < κ corresponds to a label-flip set achieving the target misclassification.

## Proposed defense or method
The paper does NOT propose a runtime defense or sanitizer. It proposes a **measurement / analysis method** (ROBUSTRANGE) that brackets the exact (NP-hard) robustness r with an efficiently computable lower and upper bound:

- **Exact formulation (baseline, exponential):** encode robustness as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) using the big-M method (Bazaraa, Jarvis, Sherali 2011). Variables: weights w_1..w_d, bias b (reals), and binary indicators δ_1..δ_m for whether each training label is flipped. Objective: minimize Σ δ_i (number of flips). Constraints force (i) the target to be classified as y_t (y_t(w·x_t + b) ≥ ε), and (ii) each training point either fit or explicitly flipped, governed by δ_i and big-M. Parameters: M ≫ 0 large constant, ε ≈ 0. Exact but does not scale.

- **Lower bound ř via partitioning (Theorem 2, ř ≤ r):** randomly partition D_c into k disjoint subsets (each ~m/k points), solve the MILP robustness per partition, and **sum** the per-partition robustness values: ř = Σ_{j=1}^k r^j. This underestimates true robustness but is far more scalable because each MILP is over a small set. Motivation: defenders care about a *guaranteed floor* on how many flips are needed (a low floor = warning of easy poisonability).

- **Upper bound r̂ via augmentation (Theorem 3, r ≤ r̂):** augment D_c with k' identical copies of the target point labeled with the adversary's desired label y_t (D'_c = D_c ∪ {(x_t, y_t)}^{k'}), then train a linear classifier on the augmented set minimizing empirical loss. This biases learning toward classifying x_t as y_t. If the learned classifier does classify x_t as y_t, the **number of misclassifications it makes on the original D_c** is an upper bound r̂ = Σ 𝕀(f_{w,b}(x_i) ≠ y_i). Fast because it only requires learning a classifier.

- **Algorithm 1 (ROBUSTNESSINTERVAL):** takes D_c, (x_t, y_t), hyperparameters M, k, k', ε, loss l; RandomPartition → per-partition MILPSolve (SCIP) → sum ř; Augment → LearnClassifier (SGDClassifier) → count misclassifications r̂; returns (ř, r̂).

## Datasets and benchmarks
Fifteen dataset/representation configurations (Table 1 rows 1–15), drawn from these sources:
- Census Income (Kohavi 1996)
- Fashion-MNIST (Xiao, Rasul, Vollgraf 2017), binarized as **Pullover vs Shirt**
- Pen-Based Handwritten Digits Recognition (Alpaydin & Alimoglu 1996), binarized as **4 vs 0** ("Digits Recognition")
- Letter Recognition (Slate 1991), binarized as **D vs O**
- Speech / hate-speech (de Gibert et al. 2018)
- SST (Socher et al. 2013)
- Emo (Yang, Xu, and Yu 2023)
- Tweet sentiment (Go, Bhayani, Huang 2009)
- Essays / Automated Essay Scoring (Hamner et al. 2012, Kaggle)
- Loan Prediction (Surana 2021, Kaggle)

Text datasets are evaluated with both **bag-of-words (BOW)** and **BERT** representations where indicated (e.g., SST(BOW)/SST(BERT), Emo, Speech, Essays, Tweet). Multi-class datasets were reduced to binary by keeping two classes. For datasets without a predefined train/test split (e.g., Census Income, Fashion-MNIST, Letter Recognition, Pen-Based Digits), **10%** of points were randomly sampled as test data and the remainder used as train. Dataset sizes: d ranges 16–784, m ranges ~1,335–80,136; test-point counts (#x_t) range from ~149 to ~8,905.

## Evaluation methodology
- Compute environment: 8-core CPU, 30 GB RAM, Ubuntu 20.04.
- MILP settings: M = 1000, ε = 10^-10.
- Partition counts k evaluated: 20, 1000, 250, 100 (varies by dataset).
- Augmentation: k' = m + 1 copies of the test point.
- Loss functions tested for l: **hinge loss, log loss, modified Huber**. For the upper bound, 10 classifiers were learned from the augmented dataset and the **minimum** robustness among them was chosen.
- Four research questions:
  - RQ1: effectiveness at finding the upper bound r̂.
  - RQ2: does poisoning up to r̂ actually impact the victim's training/predictions? Method: poison D_c by flipping labels of the r̂ points, train a classifier (all pairs of loss functions for tool vs. victim, since victim training is treated as black box), measure resulting accuracy and desired-classification frequency ρ.
  - RQ3: comparison against SOTA **IP-RELABEL** (Yang, Xu, and Yu 2023), which assumes a *white-box* victim and computes only an upper bound. For RQ2-style poisoning, poisoned sets were built by flipping labels of 0, r̂/4, r̂/2, r̂, 2r̂, 4r̂ points (extra random points for the 2r̂/4r̂ cases) and each used to train a classifier; accuracy and ρ measured; repeated over all loss-function pairs.
  - RQ4: effectiveness at finding the lower bound ř.

## Metrics
- **r̂** — average upper-bound robustness (in number of training points; also reported as % of training set).
- **ř** — average lower-bound robustness.
- **ρ** — average likelihood of achieving the desired (target) classification when poisoning per ROBUSTRANGE's bound (attack success proxy).
- **%Found (r̂_IPr)** — fraction of test points for which the SOTA IP-RELABEL tool could produce an upper bound.
- **ρ_IPr, r̂_IPr, r̂_IPr(normalized)** — the analogous success-likelihood and upper-bound columns for IP-RELABEL (normalized version assigns the training-set size as the bound when IP-RELABEL fails to produce one, for fair comparison).
- **Computation time per test point** (seconds/minutes).

## Main findings
Direct paper findings:
- **NP-Completeness (Theorem 1):** deciding whether robustness is below a threshold is NP-Complete for linear binary classifiers (reduction from Vertex Cover). This is the paper's headline theoretical result.
- **Datasets can be highly poisonable:** the authors report average robustness "can be as low as 3%" of training points; upper-bound r̂ ranged from ~1% of training points (Digits Recognition; e.g., r̂≈10.79 on m=1404) up to ~31% (Loan (BOW); r̂≈3548 on m=11200). For five datasets (Census Income, Essays BOW & BERT, Letter and Digits Recognition) r̂ was as low as 4% (or less) of training points.
- **Upper bound is fast:** average time to compute r̂ was under 1 minute per test point.
- **r̂ is a meaningful poisoning measure (RQ2):** poisoning that exceeds r̂ significantly impacts target-point classification; Figure 4 (Census Income, all 9 loss-function pairs) shows the optimal attacker outcome is at poison level = r̂ — higher values sharply reduce classifier accuracy without further increasing flip likelihood ρ, confirming r̂ as an effective threshold.
- **Beats SOTA under its own more realistic (black-box) assumption (RQ3):** IP-RELABEL (which assumes white-box victim and computes only an upper bound) produced a robustness value for **only 2 of 15 datasets**, whereas ROBUSTRANGE produced bounds for **all test points across all datasets**. Under the normalized metric ROBUSTRANGE found a **lower average robustness for 8 datasets** (tighter/lower upper bound), and in success rate ROBUSTRANGE **outperformed IP-RELABEL on 12 datasets**, including 6 where its average robustness was also lower. The authors note ROBUSTRANGE beats the SOTA tool "even when the SOTA tool knows the victim's classifier and learning procedure."
- **Lower bound (RQ4):** ROBUSTRANGE produced a non-zero average lower bound ř for **10 datasets**; average time per test point under 4 minutes. Even where the median ř is low (Census Income median ř = 0), the tool found ř > 500 for 5% of test points and non-zero ř for 15% of points — argued to be useful for individual test points.

## Negative results
- **Lower bound can collapse to zero for some datasets:** for Census Income the median lower-bound robustness ř = 0 (Figure 5), and the paper acknowledges average lower-bound robustness "can be low for some datasets." The partitioning approach necessarily under-approximates.
- **SOTA baseline largely failed to run:** IP-RELABEL could not compute an upper bound for **13 of 15 datasets** (reported as "found robustness for all points in only 2/15 datasets"). This is a negative result about the baseline, favoring the proposed method.
- No claim is made that the bounds are tight in general (only ř ≤ r ≤ r̂ is guaranteed); the gap between ř and r̂ is not characterized as small across the board.

## Limitations stated by the authors
- Scope restricted to **linear classifiers**; the concluding "Future Work" explicitly names extending the lower-bound algorithm to **non-linear classifiers** as the open direction.
- Attack model restricted to **label perturbations only** (no feature manipulation, no add/remove of points).
- The exact robustness is **NP-Complete**, so only bounds (not exact values) are computed at scale; the exact MILP "does not scale even for a small dataset."
- Lower bound via partitioning can be **low/zero** for some datasets (acknowledged).

## Additional limitations identified during review (label as REVIEWER SYNTHESIS)
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: **Directly inapplicable to modern agentic / deep-learning stacks.** The entire method depends on the hypothesis class being linear (MILP encoding, Vertex-Cover reduction, and the augmentation trick all assume sign(w^T x + b)). None of the guarantees transfer to neural networks, LLM fine-tuning, or agent memory/tool stacks, which are the Origin/Guardian threat surfaces. Value here is conceptual (a rigorous worst-case flip-count metric), not a drop-in tool.
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: **Binary-only.** All experiments binarize multi-class data to two classes; multi-class robustness is untested and the formulation is written for Y = {+1, −1}.
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: **"Robustness as low as 3%" is a per-target-point worst-case, not a whole-dataset property.** The metric is defined per target test point; headline percentages summarize averages/best cases across many target points and should not be read as "3% of labels poison the whole model."
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: **Bound tightness is dataset-dependent and unbounded a priori.** The paper guarantees ř ≤ r ≤ r̂ but gives no worst-case bound on the gap; a defender cannot know from the tool alone how close either bound is to the true r for a given point.
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: **No defense/mitigation is delivered.** The paper measures poisonability; it does not sanitize labels, detect poisoning at training time, or harden the classifier. Operational mitigation still requires separate label-sanitization / certified-defense work (which the related-work section cites, e.g., Paudice et al. 2019, Rosenfeld et al. 2020, Levine & Feizi 2020).
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: **Hyperparameter sensitivity underexplored.** Partition count k (20/100/250/1000) and MILP big-M (M=1000) materially affect scalability and lower-bound quality, but the paper does not report a systematic sensitivity analysis for how these choices trade bound tightness against runtime.

## Design implications
- Treat **label provenance as a first-class trust boundary.** The paper formalizes the intuition that when labels come from external/crowd sources, a small, targeted set of flips can flip a specific decision — relevant to any pipeline that ingests externally-labeled data (including agent memory or feedback loops that get labeled by third parties).
- A **per-decision minimal-influence metric** is a useful design primitive for contestability/audit: the same quantity that measures attacker cost also identifies the minimal label set that could have driven a specific model decision — a natural fit for an "explain/contest this decision" feature in a Guardian-style console.

## Implementation implications
- Bracketing an NP-hard security quantity with a cheap upper bound (augmentation) plus a partitioned lower bound (MILP-per-shard, summed) is a transferable engineering pattern: get a fast conservative estimate first, refine with the expensive solver only where needed.
- MILP/SCIP encodings are viable for *small linear* sub-problems but explicitly do not scale to full datasets — any implementation must shard (partition) or bound rather than solve exactly.

## Evaluation implications
- When comparing poisoning-robustness tools, **report coverage** (fraction of test points for which any bound is produced), not just the bound value — the SOTA baseline here looked competitive only on the 2/15 datasets it could actually run.
- Evaluate attacker success across **mismatched loss functions** between the measurement tool and the victim (the paper deliberately tests all loss-pairs because the victim is black-box); assuming loss-function knowledge inflates apparent robustness.
- Use the **poison-level sweep** (0, r̂/4, r̂/2, r̂, 2r̂, 4r̂) to validate that a computed threshold is the operationally meaningful one, rather than reporting a single point.

## Deployment implications
- For any production system relying on **linear** models (or linear heads/last-layer classifiers) with externally-sourced labels, a computed low r̂ for a business-critical test point (loan approval, fraud flag) is an actionable red flag that the decision is cheaply poisonable and warrants label-provenance controls.
- The method is a **pre-deployment / offline audit** tool (per-test-point MILP + training runs, ~<1 min upper bound, ~<4 min lower bound per point) — not a real-time runtime guardrail.

## Monitoring and incident implications
- The minimal-flip-set the metric identifies can seed **incident triage**: after an anomalous decision, enumerate the smallest label set whose corruption could have produced it, then audit those specific training labels' provenance.
- Low lower-bound ř for high-value target points can drive **prioritized label re-verification** and change-detection on the training corpus (watch exactly the labels most cheaply weaponizable).
- Applicability caveat for Origin/Guardian: this monitoring value is confined to linear-model decision surfaces; it does not extend to LLM/agent tool-invocation or memory-poisoning incidents without new theory.

## Applicability boundaries (where findings should / should NOT be generalized)
- SHOULD generalize to: binary linear classifiers with label-only poisoning threat; per-test-point robustness auditing; contestability tooling for linear decisions; the conceptual lesson that "attacker budget = minimal label-flip count" and that measuring it is NP-hard.
- SHOULD NOT generalize to: neural networks, deep models, LLMs, or agentic pipelines (no guarantees transfer); feature-space or backdoor/trigger poisoning (out of the label-only model); multi-class settings (untested); claims of *defense* or *certified robustness* (the paper measures, it does not defend); tightness claims (bound gap is not characterized).
- Terminology caution: the paper's "robustness" is **attacker-cost measurement**, not certified robustness in the randomized-smoothing sense (which it cites as an orthogonal line, e.g., Rosenfeld et al. 2020, Levine & Feizi 2020, Jia et al. 2022).

## Evidence strength (strong/moderate/preliminary/contested/insufficient)
- Theoretical result (NP-Completeness, Theorem 1): **strong** — standard, checkable reduction from Vertex Cover; membership and hardness both argued.
- Empirical claims (bounds computable at scale; r̂ as effective poison threshold; superiority over IP-RELABEL): **moderate** — supported across 15 dataset/representation configs with clear metrics, but confined to linear/binary settings, single hardware config, and one SOTA baseline that largely failed to run (limiting the strength of the head-to-head comparison).
- Overall for guardian-agent purposes: **moderate**, and **narrow in applicability** (linear models only).
