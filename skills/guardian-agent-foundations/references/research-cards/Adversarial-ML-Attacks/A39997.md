## Research question
Are state-of-the-art neural audio watermarking systems secure against overwriting attacks — where an adversary embeds a NEW watermark into already-watermarked audio to erase/override the original and hijack ownership — across white-box, gray-box, and black-box adversary knowledge levels?

## Problem definition
Neural audio watermarking (for AI-generated-content provenance and copyright) is optimized for robustness (survive benign processing) and imperceptibility, but security (resistance to intentional manipulation) is underexplored. Prior work covered removal and forgery attacks (Roman et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024b) and briefly noted white-box overwriting (Liu et al. 2024a). This paper gives the first systematic study of overwriting attacks across all three knowledge levels, challenging the field's implicit "security through obscurity" (secrecy of model weights) assumption via Kerckhoffs's principle.

## System or model being studied
Three representative SOTA neural audio watermarking systems, one per embedding-strategy class: AudioSeal (encoder-decoder/latent), Timbre (frequency-domain), WavMark (invertible neural network). Each has an embedder E and detector D over L-bit binary messages.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: given public watermarked audio x_w = E(x, m_owner), produce x'_w such that (i) D(x'_w) ≠ m_owner (original unrecoverable), (ii) D'(x'_w) = m'_adv (adversary's message recovered by adversary's detector), (iii) d(x'_w, x_w) ≤ ε (imperceptible). Net effect: hijack ownership / defeat provenance verification.
- Adversary knowledge: three levels —
  - White-box: full access to the original embedder E (insider / fully open-sourced model).
  - Gray-box: knows general architecture but not weights/training details; must train a surrogate (E', D').
  - Black-box: no architecture/weight knowledge; two sub-cases — query-based (limited detector-D queries) and zero-query (brute-force stacking of public/surrogate embedders).
- Adversary access: can obtain the publicly released watermarked audio and re-embed; query-based has limited API access to the original detector's output.
- Adversary capabilities: apply E directly (white-box); train surrogate embedder with a joint loss (watermark-recovery BCE + time-domain MSE + multi-resolution STFT frequency loss + adversarial/discriminator loss) (gray-box); sequential embedder composition or try-and-test with detector feedback (black-box).
- Attacker budget: query-based uses fewer than 10 detector queries and >50% fewer training iterations than zero-query; zero-query stacks up to ~3 embedders.
- Phase: deployment/inference (post-hoc attack on already-published watermarked audio).
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted (adversary embeds a chosen forged message/ownership).
- Digital / physical: digital audio.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: attacks are adaptive across knowledge levels; the victim watermarking systems have no explicit security defense to adapt.

## Trust assumptions
The provenance/ownership protocol assumes only the legitimate owner can produce a detector-verifiable watermark, and that model-weight secrecy suffices for security. The paper argues this is fragile: open-sourcing and reverse engineering are common, and surrogate models trained on different data converge to similar embedding strategies, so obscurity is not security (Kerckhoffs's principle). No secret-key mechanism is present in the studied neural systems.

## Attack or failure mechanism
- White-box: x'_w = E(x_w, m'_adv) — direct re-embedding.
- Gray-box: train surrogate E' via joint loss L_total = λw·Lw + λt·L_recont + λf·L_reconf + λadv·Ladv, then re-embed as in white-box; surrogates converge to similar embedding behavior even with different data/architecture.
- Black-box zero-query: x_w^(N) = (E_N ∘ … ∘ E_1)(x_w, m_adv) — sequentially stack N public/surrogate embedders.
- Black-box query-based: partially train candidate surrogates, embed, query original D to check if the original watermark is tampered, keep the most effective candidate and refine — limiting audio degradation to a single embedding pass.

## Proposed defense or method
None implemented. The paper argues future neural audio watermarking must incorporate explicit security mechanisms (e.g., key-based control as in traditional watermarking) and defenses against intentional manipulation, alongside robustness and imperceptibility.

## Datasets and benchmarks
Two speech datasets: LibriSpeech (~1,000 h English read speech) and VoxCeleb1 (>150,000 utterances, 1,251 speakers). All audio resampled to 16 kHz, WAV.

## Evaluation methodology
Attack all three watermarking systems under white/gray/black-box settings. Measure overwriting success and audio quality; analyze zero-query embedder stacking vs query-based try-and-test; compare query counts and training-iteration savings (Table 4, Figs 5–6).

## Metrics
- ASR (Attack Success Rate) for overwriting (original watermark no longer recoverable + adversary message recoverable).
- SNR (dB) as audio-quality/imperceptibility measure.
- Query count and training-iteration reduction (efficiency of query-based vs zero-query).

## Main findings
- Author claim: nearly 100% ASR across white-box, gray-box, and black-box settings on all three SOTA systems.
- Gray-box surrogates, though trained on different data and details, converge to similar embedding strategies — undermining the model-weight-secrecy security assumption.
- Black-box zero-query: stacking embedders raises ASR from ~30% (one embedder) to ~100% (three), but SNR drops from ~24 dB to ~20 dB (perceptual degradation).
- Query-based (<10 detector queries) matches zero-query ASR while preserving audio quality (single embedding) and cutting training iterations by >50%.

## Negative results
- Zero-query brute-force is inefficient and perceptually damaging (SNR loss, high compute) as the candidate set grows — a limitation of that specific strategy (mitigated by the query-based variant).

## Limitations stated by the authors
- Zero-query attacks scale poorly and degrade audio quality with more candidate methods.
- (Framing) the study exposes vulnerabilities but does not build or validate a defense; security must be a future design objective.

## Design implications
Watermark provenance is an integrity/authenticity asset, not merely a robustness feature. Ability to re-embed is not authority to claim ownership — verification must bind the watermark to a secret the adversary cannot reproduce (key-based), not to model-weight obscurity. Detectors that accept any well-formed watermark enable overwriting; provenance systems need to detect prior-watermark presence and reject re-embedding.

## Implementation implications
Adopt secret-key-controlled embedding/detection (Kerckhoffs-compliant), tamper-evidence for existing watermarks, and rate-limited detector APIs (query-based attacks used <10 queries). Assume open-sourced/reverse-engineered embedders enable white-box-equivalent attacks.

## Evaluation implications
Watermarking evaluations must include a security axis (removal, forgery, overwriting) across white/gray/black-box adversaries and query budgets — not only robustness to benign transforms and imperceptibility.

## Deployment implications
Do not rely on neural audio watermarks alone for copyright/provenance or AI-generated-content attribution in adversarial settings; ownership can be hijacked with near-100% success. Combine with cryptographic signing / secure provenance and detector-side query monitoring.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Monitor detector-query patterns for try-and-test overwriting; treat unexpected watermark changes as ownership-dispute/incident triggers; maintain out-of-band cryptographic provenance so a hijacked watermark does not by itself decide ownership. Audit records / provenance logs must be tamper-evident.

## Applicability boundaries
Results apply to neural audio (speech) watermarking for provenance/copyright under an adversary who can re-embed public audio. They generalize conceptually to any learned-watermark/provenance scheme (image/video/text, AI-generated-content labeling) but were measured only on three speech watermarking systems. Adjacent to agent security via the provenance/audit-record and content-authenticity angle (relevant to AI-generated-content governance), not a direct LLM/tool/prompt result.

## Evidence strength
- Moderate — systematic three-level threat modeling with consistent near-100% ASR across three SOTA systems and two datasets is convincing for the studied targets, but the targets have no explicit security defense (so results are somewhat expected), only speech audio is tested, no defense is validated, and no code is released.

## Confidence notes
High confidence that current neural audio watermarking lacks security against overwriting and that surrogate convergence undermines obscurity-based security (clearly demonstrated qualitatively and via the stacking/SNR curves). Lower confidence on exact per-system ASR/imperceptibility trade-offs, since detailed tables are truncated in the extracted text.
