## Research question
Can a corpus-poisoning attack on RAG systems be made more effective by JOINTLY optimizing gradient-based objectives across both the retriever and the generator (rather than treating them as disjoint sequential stages)?

## Problem definition
RAG couples a retriever (fetches documents from an external corpus) with a generator (LLM). Reliance on external corpora enables corpus poisoning: injecting crafted documents that, if retrieved, steer the generator to wrong/harmful/biased outputs. Prior attacks (PoisonedRAG, Phantom, LIAR) optimize retrieval and generation independently/sequentially, missing synergistic effects — suboptimal. Attackers also want stealth (inject as few poison documents as possible).

## System or model being studied
RAG pipeline: retriever E (dense encoder) selects Top-K documents for query Q; generator (LLM) produces answer conditioned on retrieved context. Retrievers: Contriever, BGE. Generators: Llama3, Qwen2; transfer target GPT-4o. The attack conceptualizes the RAG system as a single integrated optimization target.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: inject poisoned document(s) that are (a) retrieved for target queries and (b) steer the generator to a target (incorrect) answer T.
- Adversary knowledge/access: WHITE-BOX to both retriever and generator (architectures, parameters, gradients w.r.t. the optimizable poison sequence). Justified by the proliferation of open-source RAG components and used as a standard analysis paradigm (as in LIAR, Phantom); strong poison transferability is shown to relax this to gray/black-box via surrogates.
- Corpus access: GRAY-BOX — can inject a small fixed number of poison documents (typically one per target query), cannot modify/delete existing documents; can observe Top-K retrieved documents (RAG often cites sources), with synthetic-corpus variant to remove that observation need.
- Adversary capabilities: gradient-guided token optimization (GCG/MCG lineage) of an adversarial sequence S_adv (32 tokens, 64 iterations, ASCII-only) appended to a poisoned document.
- Attacker budget: minimal injected documents (stealth); optimization compute.
- Phase: deployment (corpus injection) → inference (retrieval + generation) manifestation.
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted (specific incorrect answer).
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: white-box optimization; transferability to unseen/black-box models demonstrated.

## Trust assumptions
The external corpus is treated as an untrusted, publicly editable surface (wikis, decentralized/indexed web content). Open-source RAG components let attackers replicate architectures. Cited sources make Top-K retrieval observable to the attacker.

## Attack or failure mechanism
A single poisoned document, if ranked into Top-K, both survives retrieval (high query similarity) and injects content that overrides the generator's answer. Joint optimization must combine gradients from two architecturally disparate models with mismatched vocabularies and tokenizers.

## Proposed defense or method
Joint-GCG (attack framework) unifying retriever+generator gradient attacks via three innovations:
1. Cross-Vocabulary Projection (CVP): map retriever token gradients into the generator's vocabulary space via a learned autoencoder-based embedding mapping f, solving a linear system to build projection matrix W ∈ R^{Vret×Vgen}.
2. Gradient Tokenization Alignment (GTA): use character-level gradients as a tokenizer-agnostic intermediary, then re-aggregate to the generator's token space, aligning sequence length and vocabulary dimensions.
3. Adaptive Weighted Fusion (AWF): dynamically set α (retriever vs. generator weight) using a retrieval-rank stability metric P (margin of poison similarity over top benign doc, normalized by average consecutive Top-K gaps), α = 1 − σ(P).
Joint loss: L_joint = (1−α)·I(d_p ∈ D_ret)·L_gen + α·L_ret, minimized by a GCG-style loop (gradient fusion → candidate generation/re-evaluation → greedy selection).

## Datasets and benchmarks
MS MARCO (primary in reported transfer results); Natural Questions, HotpotQA referenced. Retrievers: Contriever, BGE. Generators: Llama3, Qwen2, GPT-4o (black-box transfer). Baselines: PoisonedRAG (+GCG), LIAR, Phantom. Experiments repeated three times; S_adv length 32, 64 iterations, 128 candidates, N=16 positions.

## Evaluation methodology
Compare Joint-GCG vs. baselines on attack success and efficiency; ablate fixed-α vs. AWF (Fig. 5); measure cross-generator and cross-retriever transferability (Figs. 6–7); test batch poisoning and synthetic-corpus scenarios.

## Metrics
Attack Success Rate (ASR); ASR_ret (retrieval success) and ASR_gen (generation success); Position of Poisoned Document (P_os,p). Reported: up to 25% and ~5% average higher ASR than prior methods; 100% ASR_ret in direct attacks; up to 57% ASR on unseen models; cross-retriever transfer 96% (BGE→Contriever) and 87% (Contriever→BGE); cross-generator transfer ~41% (Llama3↔Qwen2); ~2% ASR increase on black-box GPT-4o from optimization.

## Main findings
- Author claim: first framework to unify gradient-based attacks across retriever and generator; joint optimization outperforms disjoint baselines (up to 25%, avg ~5% higher ASR).
- Poisons optimized under white-box show "unprecedented" transferability to unseen retrievers/generators, including black-box commercial GPT-4o — enabling a surrogate-based relaxation of the white-box assumption to gray/black-box deployment.
- AWF outperforms fixed weights; each component (CVP/GTA/AWF) contributes (ablations).

## Negative results
Black-box GPT-4o transfer gain is modest (~2% ASR increase over non-optimized poisons) — optimization transfer to a strong closed model is limited compared to open-model transfer. PoisonedRAG+GCG baseline fails perfect retrieval (95%/73% ASR_ret) whereas Joint-GCG attains 100% direct — reported as a comparative strength, not a Joint-GCG failure.

## Limitations stated by the authors
Ethical statement acknowledges misuse risk and advocates responsible use, audits, content filtering, and monitoring. The white-box assumption is acknowledged as strong but justified/relaxed via transfer. Full limitations largely deferred to the extended version.

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis)_
- Core threat model is white-box on both components; realism rests on open-source RAG prevalence and demonstrated transfer, but strong closed-model transfer is weak (~2%).
- AWF's stability metric assumes the attacker can observe Top-K retrieval; the synthetic-corpus variant mitigates but may reduce fidelity.
- No defense is proposed or evaluated; recommended mitigations (filtering, audits, monitoring) are untested here.
- ASCII-only 32-token adversarial suffixes may be detectable by perplexity/heuristic filters; robustness to such filters is not evaluated in the extract.
- Evaluation centers on MS MARCO / factual-QA; harmful-content or multi-hop robustness generalization is partly deferred.

## Design implications
RAG's retriever+generator should be treated as ONE attack surface: defenses that only harden retrieval OR generation miss joint-optimization poisons. Poison transferability means even black-box production RAG is exposed via surrogate optimization.

## Implementation implications
Deployers should assume publicly editable corpora are poisonable with a single stealthy document per query. Retrieval provenance/trust scoring, poison/perplexity filtering, and source citation hygiene (which also aids attackers via observability) need reconsideration.

## Evaluation implications
RAG security evaluation should include jointly-optimized, transferable poisons and measure both retrieval (ASR_ret) and end-to-end generation (ASR_gen) success, plus batch/synthetic-corpus and cross-model transfer — not single-stage attacks.

## Deployment implications
Directly relevant to production RAG (search, chatbots, code assistants, knowledge bases) using open-source components. Gray-box corpus injection into wikis/indexed web content is realistic; a small number of poison documents suffices, complicating detection.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Recommends regular audits, content filtering, and continuous monitoring. Detection signals could include anomalous high-similarity documents, ASCII-suffix/perplexity outliers, and unexpected Top-K ranking shifts — none evaluated here, so residual risk is high absent added defenses.

## Applicability boundaries
Scope: gradient-based corpus poisoning of RAG (dense retriever + LLM generator). Core to LLM/agent security (RAG is a primary agent knowledge surface). Should NOT be generalized to non-RAG LLM use, nor assumed effective against strong closed models beyond the modest demonstrated transfer, nor read as implying any specific defense works.
