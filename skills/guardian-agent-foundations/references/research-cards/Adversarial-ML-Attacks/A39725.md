## Research question
Can a merely-participating (non-server, non-unlearning) client in a federated learning (FL) system exploit the
information leaked by the federated unlearning (FU) process to reconstruct another client's unlearned data and then
retaliate against the client who requested unlearning?

## Problem definition
FU lets a client enforce its "right to be forgotten" (RTBF) by removing its contribution from the global model. The
paper identifies a new, under-studied risk: the FU process itself leaks information about the unlearned data via the
observable difference between the global model before (M_before) and after (M_after) unlearning. A client opposed to
unlearning (incentivized by disrupted training, extra overhead, and post-unlearning utility loss) can weaponize this
leakage.

## System or model being studied
Standard FL (FedAvg-style aggregation) with a subset of clients issuing unlearning requests; multiple FU methods
evaluated. Attack framework has three stages: (I) an unlearning-induced membership inference attack (U-MIA) that
trains per-class attack models on features formed by concatenating posteriors P_before||P_after from shadowed FU
processes; (II) a coarse-to-fine data-generation pipeline (coarse high-confidence candidate generation → cross-model
false-positive filtering via posterior permutation → sample-level beam-search refinement) to reconstruct the
unlearned data; (III) two retaliatory attacks — Anti-Unlearning Attack (AUA) forces the global model to relearn the
reconstructed data (defeating RTBF), and Discrimination-Unlearning Attack (DUA) injects targeted poisoning to degrade
performance on the unlearned client's data/distribution.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: retaliate against the unlearning client — either prevent successful forgetting (AUA) or induce
  targeted discrimination against the unlearned data/distribution (DUA); as a prerequisite, reconstruct the unlearned
  private data.
- Adversary knowledge: only what a normal FL participant has — its own local data, the FL model architecture, and the
  global models distributed by the server; NO external datasets, NO other clients' gradients, NO knowledge of the
  specific FU algorithm.
- Adversary access: observes global model before and after FU (justified because FU causes observable training
  interruptions / explicit server notifications; appendix argues the adversary can also detect FU and estimate
  M_after by monitoring update-consistency patterns).
- Adversary capabilities: local shadow training/simulation of FL+FU; can submit its own (benign-appearing) updates;
  cannot interfere with aggregation, cannot access other clients' local models or gradients.
- Attacker budget: behaves benignly through FL/FU; attack triggered only after receiving the post-unlearning model;
  reconstruction may span several aggregation rounds.
- Phase: deployment/maintenance (unlearning) + inference (MIA/reconstruction) + subsequent training rounds (AUA/DUA
  poisoning).
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted (against the specific unlearning client's data).
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: FU-algorithm-agnostic (does not adapt to a specific FU); not evaluated against a defender
  adapting to the retaliatory attack.

## Trust assumptions
Assumes an honest-but-curious/malicious insider client operating within protocol; assumes the server does not hide
the occurrence of FU or otherwise obfuscate before/after model deltas; assumes no differential-privacy or
gradient-obfuscation defense strong enough to suppress the before/after discrepancy signal.

## Attack or failure mechanism
The before/after model discrepancy implicitly encodes the influence of the unlearned data. The adversary shadows the
FU process on a self-constructed shadow dataset (e.g., hill-climbing high-confidence samples for tabular data),
partitions shadow data into external/unlearned/retained, queries shadow M_before/M_after to build labeled attack data
(labels 0/1/2 = external/unlearned/retained), and trains per-class U-MIA classifiers. Coarse-to-fine generation then
turns these discriminators into a generator: random high-confidence seeds → retained if U-MIA says "unlearned";
cross-model false-positive filtering permutes input posteriors to reduce single-posterior dominance (inspired by
Carlini et al.'s two-model filtering for LLM data extraction); a black-box, non-differentiable objective F(x)
(weighted pre-unlearning confidence + attack-model "unlearned" score − diversity/Jaccard-distance term) is optimized
by heuristic beam search with bit-level flips. AUA relearns reconstructed data; DUA poisons based on it.

## Proposed defense or method
None proposed. The paper explicitly evaluates leakage as a vulnerability of existing FU mechanisms.

## Datasets and benchmarks
Two tabular and two image datasets evaluated (image datasets referenced include SVHN/CIFAR-family per references;
work "primarily targets tabular datasets"); multiple FU methods and settings. Baselines B1 (naive MIA: high
pre-unlearning confidence), B2 (adds post-unlearning low confidence), and a more advanced baseline are compared.

## Evaluation methodology
Measure U-MIA accuracy as a direct quantifiable proxy for privacy leakage across FU methods/datasets; evaluate AUA by
whether the global model continues to memorize the unlearned samples (forgetting preserved or not); evaluate DUA by
accuracy drop on unlearned data vs. minor drop on general test data (targeting confirmation); compare reconstruction
against baselines B1/B2/advanced.

## Metrics
U-MIA attack accuracy (higher ⇒ greater leakage); post-attack retention/accuracy of the global model on unlearned
data (AUA: forgetting failed if memorization persists); prediction-accuracy drop on unlearned data vs. test data
(DUA targeting). (Exact numeric tables partly truncated in the excerpt.)

## Main findings
- Author claim: the unlearning-induced MIA achieves consistently high accuracy across all settings, demonstrating a
  realistic privacy leakage introduced by the FU process itself.
- Author claim: AUA prevents successful forgetting (model keeps memorizing unlearned samples) — undermining the RTBF
  privacy guarantee.
- Author claim: DUA produces a substantial accuracy drop specifically on the unlearned data with only a minor drop on
  general test data, i.e., targeted discrimination.
- Author claim: the attack is feasible from a normal client with no access to other clients' gradients — unlike
  gradient-inversion attacks that require a curious server.

## Negative results
For image tasks the adversary is assumed to maintain a local shadow set to emulate FU — an added assumption relative
to the clean tabular case, implicitly conceding tabular data is the primary, most-supported setting. (No explicit
"attack failed" result is reported in the excerpt.)

## Limitations stated by the authors
Not enumerated as an explicit limitations section in the extracted text; the discussion notes the image setting

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis — not author claims)_
- Strongest results are on tabular data; image reconstruction leans on a shadow-set assumption whose fidelity to the
  true unlearned distribution is not deeply validated in the excerpt.
- No defense is evaluated: obvious mitigations (DP unlearning, adding noise to before/after deltas, hiding FU
  occurrence, minimum-batch unlearning) are not tested, so the residual risk under standard privacy defenses is
  unquantified.
- Relies on observability of M_before and M_after; the appendix's "detect FU by monitoring consistency" claim widens
  applicability but is not the main-body evidence.
- Assumes a stable target: continual training/large client populations that dilute a single client's contribution may
  weaken the discrepancy signal — not characterized.

## Design implications
Machine-unlearning / RTBF features are a privacy attack surface, not only a privacy benefit: exposing before/after
model states to participants leaks membership and enables reconstruction. Any agent/system offering "delete my data
from the model" should treat the delta between pre- and post-deletion models as sensitive.

## Implementation implications
Consider DP or noise on published model updates around unlearning events, batching/aggregating unlearning so no
single client's removal is individually observable, restricting client visibility into inter-round model changes, and
rate-limiting/attesting client updates to blunt AUA relearning and DUA poisoning.

## Evaluation implications
FU methods should be red-teamed with unlearning-induced MIA and reconstruction as standard privacy audits (report
U-MIA accuracy), and with AUA/DUA as robustness tests — "certified removal" that is verifiable in isolation can still
leak via the removal event.

## Deployment implications
In cross-silo/cross-device FL with RTBF, an insider client is a realistic adversary; unlearning operations are a
high-risk window. Log and constrain who can observe model deltas; treat post-unlearning global models as capable of
re-memorizing removed data if adversarial updates follow.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Monitor for clients that submit updates re-introducing recently-unlearned patterns (AUA signature) or that
disproportionately degrade a departed client's distribution (DUA signature); maintain audit records of unlearning
events and subsequent update provenance for incident attribution.

## Applicability boundaries
Findings apply to FL with client-visible before/after global models undergoing FU, primarily tabular data. They
should NOT be generalized to centralized-only unlearning, to settings with strong DP on published updates, or to LLM
prompt/tool security directly. Relevance to agent security is adjacent: it is a concrete case of a legitimate
privacy/compliance mechanism (unlearning) becoming an exploitable leakage and integrity surface — a general lesson
for any "forget/delete" capability in agentic data systems.
