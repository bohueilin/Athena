## Research question
Can "adversarial missingness" (AM) attacks — steering a model's fitting process by carefully engineering which existing data entries are hidden (a crafted *missing-data* problem), rather than inserting or perturbing data — be generalized beyond the narrow prior setting (Full-Information Maximum Likelihood / FIML on Gaussian structural causal models) to attack general empirical-risk-minimization (ERM) learning under the widely used missing-data remediation strategies of complete-case analysis, mean imputation, and regression-based imputation?

## Problem definition
AM attacks (as framed by the paper): an adversary in the position of a *data supplier* selectively omits (hides) a subset of already-existing entries of a training dataset X, producing a partially observed dataset X̄, which a downstream "modeler" (victim) then processes with a missing-data remediation technique (e.g., imputation) before learning a model θ̂. The paper stresses AM is "significantly different from prior data poisoning attacks in that no malicious data [is] inserted and no data is maliciously perturbed" — the attack is *selective omission*. The authors argue standard defenses (data sanitization, outlier detection, statistically robust training) "do not apply," because AM involves no modification of observed data and can mimic naturally occurring Missing-Not-At-Random (MNAR) patterns. Stated limitation of prior work being remedied: existing AM attacks (Koyuncu et al. 2023, 2024) have "very narrow applicability," working only when the modeler uses FIML on Gaussian causal models.

## System or model being studied
- Victim ("modeler") learning pipelines over tabular data:
  - Generalized linear models — linear regression and logistic regression (GLMs) — trained by maximizing the appropriate log-likelihood (score function J(x; θ) = negative log-likelihood).
  - Missing-data remediation strategies attacked: complete-case analysis (CCA), mean imputation, regression-based (conditional-mean / linear-regression) imputation, and MICE (R-package "mice" v3.14.0).
  - Average-treatment-effect (ATE) estimators as an instance of general ERM: linear regression estimators plus neural/nonparametric estimators — T-Net, TARNet, Causal Forest (CF) — via the CATENets (v0.2.4) and R "grf" (v2.4.0) packages; doubly-robust estimation (Mayer et al. 2020) and the MIA technique for CF.
- Adversary-side machinery: a parameterized *missingness mechanism* p_{R|X}(r | x; φ) that probabilistically generates per-row missingness masks, learned via bi-level optimization (algorithm named BLAMM — Bi-level Formulation for Learning AM Mechanisms).

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: steer the modeler's learned parameters θ̂ toward a chosen adversarial target θ_α to achieve a specific malicious goal, while (a) keeping introduced missingness small and (b) limiting "auditable outcomes" — e.g., low missingness rate and minimal difference in predictive performance between models learned with and without the attack. Two concrete instantiated goals: (i) make a chosen target coefficient statistically insignificant / reverse its sign in a GLM (upper-level objective g = KL-divergence approximation between learned and adversarial GLM); (ii) manipulate an estimated ATE (upper-level objective = absolute error between target ATE and estimate).
- Adversary knowledge: assumes knowledge of the modeler's model class and missingness-handling (remediation) method. The authors report empirically that attacks "often transfer across models and mitigation strategies," partially relaxing this in practice, but the framework as designed assumes this knowledge (restated as a limitation in the Conclusion).
- Adversary access: controls the training data supplied. Some experiments restrict the adversary to modifying only a subset of rows — "partial data access" of 25% / 50% / 75% / 100%; the controlled fraction is assumed known and used to adapt the algorithm.
- Adversary capabilities: can only HIDE existing entries via a binary masking matrix R ∈ {0,1}^{N×d} (set entries to NA). No data insertion, no data perturbation. Masking of the treatment variable W is excluded in the ATE setting (covariates only).
- Budget: "modest levels of missingness (less than 20%)." Reported target-variable missingness rates 4.5%–18.1% across four datasets, except the CCA attack on ca-housing which required masking 40.2% of the target feature. In practice masking "at most two carefully selected features was sufficient."
- Box classification: white/gray-box (knows model class + remediation method; may know controlled data fraction). Attack timing: training-data-supply time (before the modeler processes data) — i.e., a training/preprocessing-time attack, not inference-time. Targeted. Digital, tabular. Non-adaptive with respect to a fixed assumed remediation, but the paper evaluates cross-strategy transfer.

## Trust assumptions
- The modeler trusts the supplied dataset's *pattern of missingness* as if it were benign / naturally occurring; there is no integrity check that missingness was adversarially engineered.
- The modeler's remediation step (CCA / imputation) is assumed to faithfully reconstruct or tolerate missing data — the attack exploits exactly this assumption.
- The paper assumes i.i.d. training samples (used in the asymptotic derivations underlying the differentiable proxies).
- Imputation-attack derivations assume the imputation mechanism is fixed a priori / independent of the exact adversarial mask in the asymptotic limit; the authors note that in practice imputation depends on the observed data, and they capture this via asymptotic forms of the imputation methods.

## Attack or failure mechanism
- Core reformulation: directly choosing an adversarial mask R is a combinatorial search over 2^{N×d} subsets. The authors replace it with learning a *probabilistic missingness mechanism* p_{R|X} that samples per-row masks, converting the attack into a continuous bi-level optimization:
  - Upper level (adversary): minimize g(θ̃; X) + λ·Ω(p_{R|X}; X), where g encodes the adversarial intent and Ω regularizes (penalizes) the missingness rate; λ trades off adversarial success vs. missingness.
  - Lower level (modeler surrogate): θ̃ = argmin of a *differentiable proxy* f̃ of the modeler's true training objective f.
- Key technical contribution enabling the attack: the modeler's true objective f under CCA / imputation is not differentiable with respect to the missingness mechanism, so the authors derive differentiable proxy objectives:
  - CCA: using the i.i.d. weak-law-of-large-numbers limit, the asymptotic CCA objective becomes a missingness-weighted expected score (Bayes' theorem), differentiable in φ and computable on finite data in time linear in N (vs. searching 2^N observed-row subsets).
  - Mean imputation: derive the asymptotic conditional-mean form of imputed values differentiable in φ; worst-case evaluation costs N·2^d summations (a large reduction from 2^{N×d}), and masking ≤2 features sufficed in practice.
  - Regression-based (linear-regression) imputation: analogous asymptotic proxy (derivation deferred to Appendix B, not in this file).
- Optimization: gradient descent on the upper-level objective; gradient through the lower-level solution via the implicit function theorem (standard), with an iterated-least-squares solver for the lower level (linear in N per iteration). Two missingness-mechanism parameterizations: (1) a one-hidden-layer neural network (100 hidden units) outputting a distribution over feasible masking patterns per instance (from Koyuncu et al. 2024); (2) a per-data-point parameter vector φ^(i) with softmax over feasible patterns.
- Failure exploited: because remediation (imputation/CCA) reconstructs or drops data based only on observed statistics, an adversary who shapes *which* entries are observed can bias those statistics, steering downstream estimates (coefficient signs/significance, ATE) while remaining within a plausible missing-data footprint.

## Proposed defense or method
This is an offense (attack) paper; it proposes no new defense. It explicitly states no defense methods exist for the AM threat model. As an exploratory check it tests one candidate borrowed from data-poisoning defense — data valuation (Lava; Just et al. 2023), which assigns utility scores using a clean validation set and discards lowest-utility examples up to a preset budget (here, applied after imputation). Results are deferred to Appendix D.1 (not present in this 8-page file), so the outcome of that defense test is not stated in the available text. Empirically, MICE was reported as the most robust remediation but still failed to protect the target variable against CCA attacks on the german-credit and diabetes datasets. The paper frames its contribution as motivating the *need* for AM defenses.

## Datasets and benchmarks
- Classification GLM targets: wine-quality; german-credit.
- Regression GLM targets: ca-housing (California housing); diabetes.
- One "highly statistically significant" feature per dataset, identified via a GLM on the complete data, chosen as the attack target coefficient.
- ATE / causal setting: Twins dataset (Louizos et al. 2017): 11,400 twin pairs, birth weights <2 kg, 30 covariates; heavier twin = treated, lighter = control; observed mortality 17.69% (control) vs. 16.08% (treatment); ground-truth ATE = −1.61%. Realistic observational subset constructed by keeping one twin per pair; 50/50 train/test split.
- 20 masking matrices sampled from both the learned and baseline mechanisms per condition; results averaged over 20 trials (GLM p-value / ℓ1 tables) and 5 trials (ATE access-regime table).

## Evaluation methodology
- GLM significance attack: identify a significant target coefficient on clean data; learn the AM mechanism; sample 20 masking matrices; the modeler fills missing data via CCA, mean imputation, or MICE; re-estimate coefficients and p-values; compare against an MCAR baseline that (asymptotically) removes the same amount of data from the same feature. Also test cross-strategy transfer (attack type vs. modeler type mismatch).
- ATE attack: introduce missingness into covariates (excluding treatment W); modeler imputes via mean or MICE (plus doubly-robust estimation); estimate ATE with linear, T-Net, TARNet, and Causal Forest estimators; compare against MCAR and an MNAR baseline (logistic function of masked values, Muzellec et al. 2020). Vary adversary data-access (25/50/75/100%). BLAMM is trained with a *linear* logistic-regression proxy and tested for transfer to nonlinear estimators.

## Metrics
- Attack success (GLM significance): "successful" if the average p-value of the target coefficient exceeds 0.05 (modeler fails to reject the null that θ_t = 0). Averaged over 20 trials; success marked with a checkmark in Table 1 / Appendix Table 8.
- Coefficient-manipulation magnitude: normalized ℓ1 norm of the gap between modeler-estimated and adversarial coefficients, ||θ̂ − θ_α||_1 / ||θ_α||_1, with standard deviation over 20 trials (Table 1).
- ATE manipulation: estimated ATE τ̂ (as a percentage) vs. ground-truth −1.61% and vs. the target of +10% (a shift the paper describes as ~700%); "bold" marks the estimate closest to the target (Table 2); averaged over 5 trials.
- Missingness rate: fraction of masked entries in the target/covariates (reported as diagnostics, not a success metric per se).

## Main findings
Direct paper findings (under the evaluated threat model and datasets):
- AM attacks succeed with modest missingness (<20% overall; 4.5–18.1% on the target feature except CCA/ca-housing at 40.2%).
- When attack type matched modeler type, the learned adversary made the target variable statistically insignificant in ALL evaluated cases (GLM p-value > 0.05). The MCAR baseline was unsuccessful in ALL cases.
- Twins/ATE: the attack reversed the sign and substantially inflated magnitude of the estimated ATE — from a true −1.61% to as high as +10% — across multiple regression-based estimators (linear, T-Net, TARNet, CF), even when the adversary could modify only a subset of rows.
- Transfer / generalization (Table 2): a BLAMM mechanism trained against a simple *linear* logistic-regression proxy remained effective against *nonlinear* estimators (T-Net, TARNet, Causal Forest), and effective under reduced access down to 25% (expected masked fractions 7.2% / 11.1% / 24.3% / 12.5% for 100/75/50/25% access). Attacks optimized against a mean-imputation proxy still inflated the ATE even when the modeler used MICE or the MIA technique for CF. In contrast, MCAR and MNAR baselines left the ATE estimate close to the ground truth.
- Cross-strategy asymmetry (Table 1): under attack/modeler mismatch, CCA-designed attacks generally succeeded at manipulating a mean-imputation modeler, while mean-imputation-designed attacks showed limited generalization to other modelers.
- MICE was the most robust imputation method tested but still failed to prevent the target variable from becoming insignificant against CCA attacks on german-credit and diabetes.

## Negative results
- Mean-imputation attacks showed "limited generalization" when the modeler's remediation differed from the one the attack was designed against (attack/modeler mismatch).
- MICE resisted several attacks (most robust of the imputation methods) yet did not withstand CCA attacks on german-credit and diabetes.
- MCAR and MNAR baselines were NOT effective attacks — they left estimates near ground truth — establishing that mere randomly/naively missing data does not reproduce the effect; the adversarial *engineering* of the mechanism is what matters.
- Efficiency caveat presented as a limitation of the mean-imputation proxy: worst-case N·2^d summations (mitigated by masking ≤2 features in practice).

## Limitations stated by the authors
- The framework assumes knowledge of the model class and the missingness-handling method used by the modeler; the authors note only "some evidence" that attacks designed for one mitigation transfer to others.
- Experiments target tabular data; extensions to other modalities (deep nets, images) and efficiency improvements are deferred to future work.
- Remediation strategies not yet covered: multiple imputation, k-nearest-neighbor imputation, and generative-model-based imputation (listed as promising future directions).
- The mean-imputation proxy has exponential (2^d) worst-case cost in the number of masked features; the authors rely on masking few (≤2) features.
- No defense exists for the AM threat model; the authors tested only a single borrowed data-valuation defense (results in Appendix D.1, outside this file).

## Additional limitations identified during review (REVIEWER SYNTHESIS)
- The following are reviewer observations, NOT paper claims:
  - Success criterion for the GLM attack (p-value > 0.05) measures the modeler's *failure to detect* significance, not a bounded control over the coefficient value; the ℓ1 metric complements it, but "success" as tabulated is a hypothesis-test outcome that can be sensitive to sample size and the chosen 0.05 threshold.
  - Evaluation is on modest-dimensional tabular datasets with a single chosen target coefficient; generalization to high-dimensional feature sets, many simultaneous targets, or heavily correlated covariates is not demonstrated here.
  - The strong transfer claims rest on a specific set of estimator implementations (CATENets, grf, mice) at pinned versions; robustness to other imputation libraries, cross-validated/regularized fitting, or modelers who run missingness-pattern diagnostics is not evaluated.
  - The threat model's plausibility hinges on the adversary occupying a trusted data-supplier role and on the injected missingness being indistinguishable from natural MNAR; the paper asserts detectability is low but does not report a detector's ROC/AUC against the learned mechanism (only that MCAR/MNAR baselines are ineffective as attacks — a different question from detectability of the attack).
  - Appendices B/C/D (proxy derivations for regression imputation, the BLAMM algorithm listing, and full experimental/defense results including the data-valuation defense) are absent from this camera-ready file, so several quantitative claims cannot be fully verified from the local source alone; they require the arXiv:2409.04407 extended version.

## Design implications
- For systems that ingest externally supplied training or fine-tuning data, treat the *pattern of missingness* as an attackable, integrity-relevant signal — not just the present values. A supplier who can only decide what to omit can still steer downstream estimates.
- Pipelines that silently impute or drop missing rows create an exploitable trust gap; design should record and expose the missingness footprint (per-feature/per-source rates, MNAR indicators) as first-class metadata rather than discarding it during remediation.
- Relevance to the Guardian/Origin data-supply-chain posture: this is an integrity threat that bypasses classic poisoning defenses (sanitization, outlier detection) because nothing is inserted or perturbed — argues for provenance/attestation on *complete-record* supply, not just anomaly filtering on present values.

## Implementation implications
- Imputation choice matters as a (partial) hardening lever: MICE was the most robust of the tested methods, but not sufficient — do not treat any single imputer as a defense.
- If a supplier controls even 25% of rows, engineered missingness can dominate downstream estimates (Twins ATE result); systems aggregating data from multiple sources should bound and monitor any single source's influence, especially its contribution to missingness.
- Because attacks trained on a simple linear proxy transferred to nonlinear estimators, using a more complex model class is NOT a reliable mitigation.

## Evaluation implications
- When red-teaming data pipelines, include *omission-based* attacks, not only insertion/perturbation poisoning; and include an MCAR (and MNAR) baseline to confirm that any observed manipulation is due to engineered structure rather than random missingness.
- Use both a detection-oriented metric (does the target's significance/sign flip?) and a magnitude metric (normalized ℓ1 to a target) to characterize an omission attack; significance-flipping alone can understate or overstate control.
- Evaluate cross-strategy transfer explicitly: an attack designed against one remediation (e.g., CCA) may succeed against another (e.g., mean imputation), per the paper's asymmetry finding.

## Deployment implications
- Deployments that rely on statistical significance, coefficient signs, or treatment-effect estimates from supplier-provided data (e.g., policy, pricing, causal/impact analysis, EHR/insurance/census-style tabular pipelines) are exposed to silent manipulation via engineered missingness with a small (<20%) footprint.
- No production-ready defense is validated here; any mitigation (missingness auditing, provenance, source-influence caps, data valuation) requires production validation before being relied upon.

## Monitoring and incident implications
- Instrument and alert on missingness structure: per-source and per-feature missingness rates, sudden concentration of missingness in high-leverage/significant features, and divergence from expected MAR patterns. The paper shows attacks concentrate on ≤2 carefully chosen features — a monitorable signature.
- Track downstream estimate stability: unexplained flips in coefficient significance/sign or large ATE swings correlated with a specific data source are candidate incident signals.
- Note the paper does not provide a detector; these are mitigations to build and validate, not proven controls.

## Applicability boundaries (where findings should / should NOT be generalized)
- SHOULD generalize (within evidence): to supervised/unsupervised learning with differentiable ERM losses on tabular data using CCA / mean / regression-based imputation (and, with caveats, MICE); to GLM significance manipulation and regression-based ATE estimation; to settings where an adversary is a trusted or partial data supplier able to omit existing entries.
- SHOULD NOT be assumed (not evaluated here): LLMs, agents, prompt/tool/skill injection, or any non-tabular modality (images, deep nets) — explicitly deferred to future work; multiple imputation, k-NN imputation, and generative-model imputation — not covered; high-dimensional / many-target / heavily-correlated regimes; modelers who audit missingness patterns or enforce data provenance; the efficacy of the tested data-valuation defense (result not in this file).
- The paper's claims are "demonstrated under the evaluated threat model" (known model class + remediation, tabular data, chosen target); they are not evidence of an unconditional or model-agnostic capability.

## Evidence strength (strong/moderate/preliminary/contested/insufficient)
Moderate. The attack framework is principled (bi-level optimization with derived differentiable proxies) and results are consistent across four tabular datasets, multiple remediation strategies, multiple ATE estimators, and reduced data-access regimes, with appropriate MCAR/MNAR baselines and multi-trial averaging. Strength is bounded by: tabular-only, single-target-coefficient evaluation; the assumption of known model class + remediation; reliance on a p-value>0.05 success criterion; and the fact that key derivations/experiment details and the sole defense test live in an extended version absent from this file. No defense is validated. Claims should be read as demonstrated under the specified threat model, not as general-purpose or production-validated.
