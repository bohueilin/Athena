This is a **game-theoretic mechanism-design / algorithmic-economics** paper on optimal audit-policy design against *strategically adversarial* (self-interested, misreporting) agents under worst-case equilibrium selection. It is NOT about adversarial examples, evasion attacks, poisoning, or perturbation-based ML attacks. "Adversarial" here means agents who game a stated policy and collectively pick the equilibrium worst for the auditor.

## Research question
How should a principal design an optimal audit policy to elicit truthful reports from a population of self-interested agents who may misreport private types to gain benefits/access, when those agents adversarially (pessimistically) select the *worst-case* equilibrium for the principal? The paper analyzes this systematically across three axes: (1) objective — maximize principal's own utility vs. social welfare; (2) adaptivity — a fixed audit vector vs. a policy responsive to the observed report distribution; (3) resources — a per-audit marginal cost vs. a hard audit budget.

## Problem definition
An audit-mechanism-design problem framed as a principal–(multi-)agent Stackelberg game. A principal interacts with a continuum of non-atomic agents of total mass n. Each agent has a private type i drawn i.i.d. from a known prior q over m ≥ 2 ordered types [m] = {0,…,m−1}, and may report a possibly different type k. When a type-i agent reports k, the principal assigns payment pay(k) and receives value val(i,k). The principal detects misreporting by choosing an audit vector p ∈ [0,1]^m, where p_k is the probability of auditing an agent that reports k; an audited misreporter incurs a type-independent penalty pen(i,k) = 1[i≠k]·pen(k). Each audit costs λ ≥ 0 (costly setting) or is constrained by budget B (budgeted setting — deferred to the full version, not fully contained in this PDF). The principal commits first to the audit policy; agents then collectively choose the equilibrium that minimizes the principal's utility. The principal wants to maximize her worst-case (pessimistic) equilibrium utility (or social welfare).

## System or model being studied
A Stackelberg (leader–follower) game with one leader (the principal/auditor) and a continuum of followers (agents). Because agents are non-atomic, the induced report distribution equals its expectation, and equilibrium is a Wardrop-style equilibrium (a single agent's deviation does not change the aggregate report distribution). Motivating real-world systems: the U.S. Social Security Administration Supplemental Security Income (SSI) asset-limit eligibility check, tax-compliance auditing, and lending/credit decisions — all resource-allocation settings where agents may misreport to obtain benefits. Robustness notion is standard pessimistic/robust equilibrium (Conitanti/Coniglio-Gatti-Marchesi 2017-style pessimistic Stackelberg).

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: agents individually maximize their own expected utility U_{i,k}(p) = pay(k) − p_k·pen(i,k); collectively they select the Bayes–Nash equilibrium that is *worst for the principal* (adversarial / pessimistic equilibrium selection). This is the paper's core adversary model — "agents adversarially select the worst-case equilibrium."
- Adversary knowledge: full knowledge of the principal's committed audit vector p (non-adaptive) or audit strategy π (adaptive), plus the payment/value/penalty structure — effectively white-box with respect to the announced mechanism. Agents also know the shared prior q; equilibrium is over the induced report distribution.
- Adversary access/capabilities: agents choose only their *reported* type (a report strategy Q, the probability a type-i agent reports type k). They cannot alter payments, penalties, audit probabilities, or the audit itself; the only lever is misreporting.
- Adversary budget: none imposed on agents. On the principal side, audit cost λ per audit (or budget B in the deferred budgeted setting).
- Taxonomy placement: this is *not* the standard white/gray/black-box perturbation taxonomy. Mapping loosely: white-box w.r.t. the announced policy; deployment-/mechanism-time strategic manipulation (not training- or inference-time perturbation of an ML model); targeted in the sense of adversarial equilibrium selection; digital/economic (no physical channel); adaptive in the game-theoretic sense (best-response to the committed policy) and, in §3.2, over T rounds against an online learner without a prior.

## Trust assumptions
- Prior q over types is known to the principal in the non-adaptive costly setting (Theorem 1), the social-welfare setting (Theorem 3), and the adaptive setting (Theorem 4). The no-regret online algorithm (§3.2, Theorem 2) relaxes this: q is unknown and can vary each round, chosen by Nature (secretly) before each round.
- Audits are assumed to perfectly reveal an agent's true type when performed (the main model detects any misreport when audited). Footnote 1 notes a weaker "type-independent" penalty can model auditing that only detects inconsistency between reported and true types without identifying the true type, but the main analysis assumes reliable detection.
- Structural assumptions on the game: penalty is type-independent, pen(k); val(i,k) monotone so that "misreporting higher only decreases the principal's value" (Eq. 1); no-incentive-to-misreport-if-audited pen(k) ≥ pay(k) (Eq. 2); audit cost below penalty λ ≤ pen(k) (Eq. 5); and, for the adaptive results, penalty is "less sensitive than payment," pay(l)/pay(k) ≥ pen(l)/pen(k) (Eq. 12), satisfied by any positive affine penalty pen(k)=a·pay(k)+b, a,b ≥ 0.
- Agents can coordinate to reach the specific worst-case equilibrium — a strong-adversary assumption that makes the guarantees robust but potentially pessimistic.
- The predictive/allocation rule that maps applicants to eligibility (the "classifier") is taken as exogenous and fixed; the paper designs only the audit policy on top of it.

## Attack or failure mechanism
The failure being defended against is strategic misreporting (fraud) in resource allocation: agents under-report private information (e.g., assets below the SSI $2,000 "deemed resources" limit, illicit export turnover, evaded tolls, income) to obtain benefits or access they are not entitled to. Because multiple equilibria can exist, the adversarial agents coordinate on the equilibrium that most damages the principal — which may be a *non-truthful* equilibrium. A second, structural "failure" the paper proves: even with full knowledge, the principal cannot always achieve an exactly optimal policy — Proposition 1 exhibits a nonadaptive costly audit game where the supremum of the principal's worst-case utility over all audit vectors is finite but is *not attained* by any p (the optimum is unattainable; e.g., the illustrated binary-type game has worst-case utility 1/4 at p1=1/4 but sup_p V_λ = 15/8 approached only as p1→1/4⁺).

## Proposed defense or method
Algorithms that compute (near-)optimal audit policies with provable guarantees under the pessimistic equilibrium:
- Characterize agent best response as a threshold structure (Lemma 1): every agent is either truthful or misreports as the highest-utility type; there is a minimal truthful type i_truth, below which agents misreport within a "misreporting range."
- Define "equalized" and "critical" audit vectors (Definition 2) — a finite, structured search space of O(m²) candidate vectors that provably approximate any audit vector (Lemmas 2–5).
- Theorem 1 / Algorithm 1 (SuccinctSearch): computes a 2nε-optimal audit vector for the principal's worst-case utility (Eq. 7) in O(m⁴) time, improvable to O(m²) via dynamic programming of the inner ComputeVal routine. Proposition 1 proves exact optimality is impossible in general, justifying the ε-approximation.
- Theorem 2 / Algorithm 2 (EXP3 over critical audit vectors): a no-regret online-learning auditor for the prior-free setting, achieving multi-agent Stackelberg regret O(n·√(T·m²·log m)) over T rounds. Key insight: although exact optimality is impossible, restricting arms to the prior-independent critical audit vectors preserves the no-regret property.
- Theorem 3: a 2nε-optimal audit vector for *social welfare* in O(m²) time (nearly identical algorithm; a no-regret variant also applies).
- Proposition 2 (monotonicity): increasing the penalty function or decreasing the audit cost λ can only (weakly) improve both the principal's worst-case utility and social welfare; intuitively, a higher penalty lets the auditor lower audit probabilities p_k = pen(k)/pen'(k)·p_k while preserving equilibria, reducing audit cost.
- Theorem 4: for adaptive audits under the insensitivity assumption (Eq. 12), adaptive policies offer *no advantage* over non-adaptive ones (sup_π min_Q V_λ = sup_p min_Q V_λ); an ε-optimal adaptive policy is computable in O(m²). Dictator audit strategies (audit everyone if reports differ from target, else no one) plus single-minded equilibria underpin this result (Lemmas 6–7).

## Evaluation methodology
Primarily theoretical: formal theorems with correctness/approximation and runtime guarantees; regret analysis for the online setting; existence/impossibility results (Proposition 1, Theorem 4). Most proofs are deferred to a "full version" not contained in this 8-page proceedings PDF. Empirical component is illustrative simulation only (§5): computing the optimal policy and resulting equilibria on small 3-type examples to visualize how the optimum, the worst-equilibrium region, and truth-telling regions depend on the prior q (Fig. 2) and the payment function (Fig. 3). No adversarial-robustness evaluation, no ASR/defense-rate tables, no baselines beyond the theoretical comparison of adaptive vs. non-adaptive and principal-utility vs. social-welfare policies.

## Metrics
- Principal's worst-case (pessimistic) equilibrium utility V_λ(p) = min_{Q∈Eqi(p)} V_λ(p,Q).
- Social welfare W_λ(p,Q) (sum of principal and agent utilities net of audit cost).
- Approximation quality: ε / 2nε-optimality (an audit vector is ε-optimal if V_λ(p) ≥ sup_{p'} V_λ(p') − ε).
- Multi-agent Stackelberg regret Reg_T(A) over T rounds (Eq. 10).
- Computational-complexity metrics: O(m²), O(m⁴) runtime; O(n·√(T·m²·log m)) regret.
- Simulation visualizations: color-encoded principal utility / social welfare over the prior simplex and over payment values; truth-telling vs. worst-equilibrium regions.

## Main findings
- Full characterization of the equilibrium structure in the non-adaptive costly setting, with an ε-approximation algorithm running in O(m²) (Theorem 1); exact optimality is provably impossible in general (Proposition 1).
- A no-regret online auditor exists even without a prior, with regret O(n·√(T·m²·log m)) (Theorem 2); careful arm selection (critical audit vectors) recovers no-regret despite the impossibility of exact optimality.
- Both principal-utility and social-welfare objectives are handled with the same algorithmic machinery (Theorem 3).
- Monotonicity (Proposition 2): raising penalties or lowering audit cost weakly benefits both the principal's utility and social welfare.
- Adaptive audits give no advantage over non-adaptive audits under the insensitivity assumption Eq. 12 (Theorem 4) — a notable structural simplification, meaning a committed fixed audit vector suffices in that regime.
- Simulation observations: the principal-optimal policy is *stricter* than the welfare-optimal one and yields a larger truth-telling region, because misreports impose greater costs on the principal than on overall welfare (Fig. 2); the effect of the payment function is non-monotone — increasing payments can decrease or increase welfare depending on the prior (Fig. 3).

## Negative results
- Proposition 1: there exists a non-adaptive costly audit game whose principal worst-case utility has a finite supremum over audit vectors that is *not attained* by any p — exact optimal auditing is impossible; only ε-approximation is achievable.
- Theorem 4: adaptive (report-distribution-responsive) audit policies provide *no improvement* over non-adaptive ones under the insensitivity assumption Eq. 12 — a negative result for the value of adaptivity in that regime.

## Limitations stated by the authors
From the Discussion and Future Work section:
- Guarantees are proven for a *continuum* of agents; extending them to *finite* agent populations is left open.
- The model assumes reliable audits; extending to *noisy or partial verification* remains open.
- Richer *penalty structures* (beyond type-independent / affine) are not covered.
- The predictive classifier / allocation rule is treated as *exogenous*; jointly designing the prediction model and the audit policy could yield better performance and is future work.
- Extending the incentive-minimization framework of Estornell, Chen, and Das (2023) to *non-binary payment outcomes* is future work.
- (Implicit in the paper) the budgeted-audit setting and several proofs are deferred to a "full version" not included here.

## Additional limitations identified during review (REVIEWER SYNTHESIS)
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: The corpus category "Adversarial-ML-Attacks" is a mislabel. The paper contains no ML attack, no perturbation/evasion/poisoning, and no ML model under attack; "adversarial" denotes strategic economic agents and worst-case equilibrium selection. Findings should not be cited as evidence about adversarial-example robustness of ML systems.
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: The non-atomic/continuum abstraction removes individual-agent granularity and small-population/collusion dynamics; real audited populations are finite and may deviate from Wardrop-equilibrium predictions.
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: The worst-case-equilibrium-selection adversary is very strong (assumes perfect agent coordination on the least-favorable equilibrium). Guarantees are therefore conservative; in practice agents may not coordinate this well, so the model may over-provision audits.
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: The prior q is assumed known in all closed-form results except the online no-regret setting, whose regret bound scales linearly in agent mass n and as √T — meaningful only over many rounds.
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: Assumptions Eq. 1/2/5/12 (monotone value, penalty ≥ payment, cost ≤ penalty, penalty less sensitive than payment) are load-bearing — e.g., the adaptive-equals-non-adaptive result requires Eq. 12; outside these assumptions the conclusions may not hold and the optimal equilibrium may not be single-minded (noted after Lemma 7).
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: Empirical validation is limited to hand-picked 2- and 3-type synthetic examples; there is no evaluation on real SSI/tax/lending data despite those being the motivating applications.
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: Audits are assumed to detect misreporting with certainty; real-world verification error (false positives/negatives) is unmodeled in the main results and could materially change incentives.

## Design implications
- For any system that grants benefits/access on the basis of self-reported private information (agentic or human), a *committed, published* audit policy plus a penalty for detected misreporting can be designed to make truthful reporting an equilibrium — and, under Eq. 12, a *fixed* (non-adaptive) audit vector is as good as an adaptive one, simplifying design.
- Design to the *worst-case equilibrium*, not the average or best case: multiple equilibria can exist and strategic parties may coordinate on the one that hurts the operator most. This mirrors robust/pessimistic-Stackelberg thinking useful when reasoning about coordinated agent behavior in a Guardian/PolicyGuard-style enforcement layer.
- Penalty strength and audit cost are the key tuning knobs: raising penalties or lowering per-check cost weakly improves both operator utility and social welfare (Proposition 2), so cheaper/automated verification is strictly helpful design leverage.

## Implementation implications
- The optimal policy lives in a small, structured search space (O(m²) "critical/equalized" audit vectors), so computing near-optimal audit probabilities is cheap even at scale — practical for a runtime audit/verification controller that must recompute policies as parameters change.
- When the population prior is unknown or drifting, an EXP3-style online learner over the fixed set of critical audit vectors gives a no-regret auditing controller (Algorithm 2) — a concrete pattern for adaptive-but-bounded audit-rate control without needing an accurate prior.
- These are *approximation* algorithms (2nε-optimal); implementers should treat ε as a tunable accuracy/cost knob and not expect an exactly optimal policy (Proposition 1 rules that out).

## Evaluation implications
- Evaluating an audit/verification mechanism should report *worst-case* equilibrium performance, not just the intended (truthful) equilibrium, since adversarial parties may select a different equilibrium.
- Sweeping the prior and the payment/penalty structure (as in Figs. 2–3) is a reasonable way to visualize truth-telling vs. worst-equilibrium regions; note the payment effect can be non-monotone, so single-point evaluations can mislead.
- REVIEWER SYNTHESIS: absence of real-data or finite-population evaluation means external validity for deployed benefit/credit systems is unestablished and would require production validation.

## Deployment implications
- Directly relevant to deployed high-stakes allocation systems (SSI eligibility, tax compliance, lending) where humans conduct a limited number of audits flagged by an AI — the paper formalizes how to allocate that scarce audit capacity against strategic misreporting.
- For agentic-AI settings, the framing transfers to a principal committing to an audit/verification policy over autonomous agents that self-report state to gain access/credit; the pessimistic-equilibrium guarantee is attractive for adversary-robust deployment, but the strong-coordination adversary and continuum assumptions mean real deployments need validation of whether agents actually reach the modeled worst equilibrium.
- The budgeted (hard audit-cap) variant — often the real operational constraint — is only sketched here (deferred to the full version), so deploying under a strict audit budget requires that additional material.

## Monitoring and incident implications
- The audit vector p is itself a monitoring-rate policy: p_k is the probability of verifying a report of type k. This maps cleanly onto risk-based sampling in a monitoring pipeline (audit high-risk report classes more heavily).
- Detected misreports should carry a real, enforced penalty (pen(k) ≥ pay(k)) for the incentive math to hold; a monitoring system that flags but does not penalize does not achieve the truthful equilibrium.
- Monitoring should watch for *shifts in the report distribution* (the observed q̂), since equilibria depend only on the induced report distribution; under Eq. 12, however, responding adaptively to that shift yields no gain over a fixed policy (Theorem 4) — useful to avoid over-engineering an adaptive monitor.

## Applicability boundaries (where findings should / should NOT be generalized)
- SHOULD generalize to: strategic-reporting / verification-under-cost settings with a committing principal, a large (near-continuum) population, reliable audits, monotone value, and penalty ≥ payment — benefit eligibility, tax audit, lending/credit verification, and analogous agent-self-attestation flows.
- SHOULD NOT be generalized to: adversarial-example / evasion / poisoning ML robustness (despite the corpus label), finite/small populations without re-derivation, settings with noisy or partial verification, non-affine or type-dependent penalties, or cases violating the insensitivity assumption Eq. 12 (where adaptive audits *can* help and the optimum may not be single-minded).
- The exact-optimality impossibility (Prop 1) and the adaptive=non-adaptive equivalence (Thm 4) are specific to the stated assumptions and should not be quoted as universal.
