## Research question
Can a game-theoretic AI defense agent compute optimal deception-resource (e.g., honeypot) deployment strategies to counter AI-empowered cyberattacks (such as LLM-driven Advanced Persistent Threats), where static deception defenses fail against continuously adapting AI attackers?

## Problem definition
Traditional deception defense (DD) — decoys/honeypots to waste attacker resources and reveal TTPs — is discounted against intelligent, adaptive attacks (APTs), amplified by an inherent asymmetry (attacker must succeed once; defender must succeed every time). AI-powered attacks lower the barrier to one-time success and continuously evolve via reconnaissance, while existing attacker-modeling remains manual and static. The proposal aims to deploy "AI against AI" for adaptive deception strategy.

## System or model being studied
A proposed (not-yet-evaluated) game-theoretic AI defense framework. The defender–attacker interaction is formalized as a dynamic game with incomplete information G(N, A, T, O, P, R): players N = {A_ID defender, A_IA attacker} as AI-driven adaptive agents (potentially LLMs fine-tuned on cybersecurity data); action space A (defender = DD-resource deployment/adjustment, attacker = adaptive attack behaviors); type space T (private attributes, e.g., defender budget ∈ {High, Medium, Low}); observation space O (noisy, incomplete); probability space P (type prior, observation noise, transitions) with Bayesian belief updates; payoff R balancing detection gains, resource cost, and time. An architecture (Fig. 1) adds a Storage Module (cached pre-computed strategies) and Computing Module, with a human expert making the final DD-deployment decision (human-in-the-loop).

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: succeed at a cyber intrusion / APT progression while avoiding deception (honeypots), minimizing cost of avoidance.
- Adversary knowledge (white/gray/black-box): incomplete-information game — the attacker acts on limited, noisy knowledge of network state; both sides hold private "types." Gray-box in spirit.
- Adversary access: network reconnaissance and interaction with (possibly decoy) systems; observes defender actions before choosing its own.
- Adversary capabilities: AI/LLM-empowered — autonomous network probing, phishing generation, adaptive evasion based on reconnaissance (e.g., avoiding low-fidelity honeypots).
- Attacker budget: modeled abstractly via cost terms in the payoff; no concrete budget figures.
- Phase: deployment / runtime network defense (ongoing attacker–defender interaction).
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted intrusion modeled as strategic play.
- Digital / physical: digital (network cyber operations).
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: explicitly adaptive on both sides — the central premise is countering an adaptive AI attacker via equilibrium strategies.

## Trust assumptions
The defender's AI framework and its human expert are trusted; the human makes the final deployment decision (the AI provides strategy recommendations, not autonomous action). Assumes the interaction can be validly captured as a dynamic Bayesian game and that equilibrium strategies transfer to real adversaries. Payoff/cognitive-model specifics are unspecified ("determined once the cognitive model is finalized").

## Attack or failure mechanism
AI-empowered attackers reduce attack cost, expand scope, and increase sophistication (LLMs generating phishing, autonomously probing networks). Against static DD they adapt around fixed decoys; the asymmetry means a single success suffices. The defense failure being addressed is the static, non-adaptive nature of conventional honeypot deployment.

## Proposed defense or method
Model attacker–defender as a dynamic incomplete-information game between AI agents; derive equilibrium defense strategies via cognitive modeling and Bayesian belief updates; accelerate real-time response by caching pre-computed strategy combinations in a Storage Module (fallback to a Computing Module when no cached strategy exists); keep a human expert in the loop for final deployment. Claimed benefits: higher strategic intelligence (AI-vs-AI), better real-time responsiveness (pre-computation), and potential scalability (lightweight design).

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis — not author claims)_ No implementation, no experiments, no metrics — all claims (scalability, real-time capability, strategic intelligence) are aspirational. Solving/approximating equilibria in large dynamic incomplete-information games is computationally hard; the Storage-Module caching assumes a tractable, reusable strategy space that is not demonstrated. Modeling a real AI attacker's "type"/cognition is a strong assumption; equilibrium optimality depends on model fidelity that may not hold against out-of-model adversaries. No treatment of the attacker corrupting the defender's own AI (e.g., poisoning, prompt injection of the defender LLM). Human-in-the-loop latency may conflict with the real-time claim. "Highly likely in real-world" attack scenarios are assumed, not characterized.

## Design implications
Framing adaptive cyber defense as an incomplete-information game between *AI* agents is a useful conceptual lens for the "AI-vs-AI" arms race. The human-in-the-loop-decides pattern (AI recommends deception strategy, human authorizes deployment) aligns with "Models propose; humans/gates decide" — capability separated from authority. Pre-computing/caching strategies is a reasonable latency-mitigation idea for gate-time decisions.

## Implementation implications
An equilibrium-strategy cache keyed on observed attacker signals could serve as a fast policy lookup, but requires a tractable strategy representation and a fallback solver — both unbuilt here. Any defender that is itself an LLM/AI agent becomes a new attack surface (defender-side poisoning/injection) that this design does not yet address.

## Evaluation implications
Claims of scalability/real-time/strategic-intelligence must be measured against a traditional-DD control under realistic AI-driven attacks before acceptance; the proposed metric set (TTP-detection degree, defense success %, resource cost, response time) is a sensible starting battery. Synthetic-only validation would be insufficient for the real-world adaptivity claim.

## Deployment implications
Not deployable as described — proposal stage. If pursued, deception deployment should remain human-authorized, and the defender AI's own integrity (against reconnaissance, poisoning, and manipulation) must be part of the threat model.

## Monitoring and incident implications
The framework's observation space and Bayesian belief updates are, in effect, a monitoring/attribution mechanism (e.g., inferring attacker "Advanced" type when it avoids low-fidelity honeypots). TTP-detection metrics could feed an incident-response loop, but no logging/evidence design is specified.

## Applicability boundaries
Conceptually relevant to autonomous/AI-driven cyber defense and to agent-security threat modeling (adaptive adversaries, incomplete information, AI-vs-AI). Should NOT be cited as evidence that game-theoretic deception *works* against AI attackers — there are no results. Confined to network deception defense; does not address application-layer LLM/agent attacks (prompt injection, tool abuse) except by loose analogy. Treat as an agenda, not a validated method.
