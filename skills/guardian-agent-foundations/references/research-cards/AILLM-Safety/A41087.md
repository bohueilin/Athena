## Research question
What is the minimum number of preference-label flips an adversarial annotator must make during RLHF/DPO alignment to steer an LLM's optimal policy toward an attacker-specified target policy, without altering the compared outputs — and can existing label-flipping attacks be post-processed to reach that minimum?

## Problem definition
Preference-alignment pipelines (pre-training → SFT → RLHF/DPO) are vulnerable to data poisoning. The realistic, constrained attack is a malicious annotator who can flip only the binary preference label w for triplets (x, y, z) but cannot modify the context or outputs. Prior work shows this vulnerability empirically; the theoretical minimum attack cost was unquantified.

## System or model being studied
RLHF/DPO reward-model learning. Reward modeled as r(x,y)=r^T φ(x,y) with a fixed or adaptive embedding φ (baseline LLM with unembedding layer removed); human preference via Bradley-Terry; the LM policy is the KL-regularized optimal policy under the learned reward (DPO equivalence used). Two settings analyzed: fixed embedding and adaptive (trainable) embedding.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: steer the victim's optimal policy to a target policy π_A (equivalently, steer the reward model to a target reward r_A) at minimum label-flip cost.
- Adversary knowledge: has access to the labeled preference dataset D_L; target reward may be handcrafted or produced by prior attacks (Wu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024) under white-box or black-box assumptions.
- Adversary access: acts as an annotator; can set/flip preference labels w_i ∈ {−1,1}.
- Adversary capabilities: label flipping only — cannot modify (x, y, z); idealized analysis allows continuous control of annotation probability η ∈ [0,1].
- Attacker budget: the object of study — number of label flips (ℓ1 cost) or a general norm ‖θ_A − θ_O‖.
- Phase: training (RLHF/DPO alignment / preference optimization).
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted (specific target policy/reward).
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: post-processing adapts/optimizes any given attack to lower cost; theory is worst-case over feasible datasets.

## Trust assumptions
Assumes some annotations come from an untrusted annotator; assumes the reward model form r=r^Tφ and (for fixed-embedding results) Assumption 1 (φ(x,y)−φ(x,z) ∈ col(Φ), naturally satisfied when feature dim n < dataset size N); assumes strong duality via a relaxed Slater condition (feasible solution ζ=θ_A−θ_O exists).

## Attack or failure mechanism
Because many reward functions induce the same optimal policy (r and r+R(x) are equivalent), the attacker has freedom to choose among reward functions in R(π_{r_A}). Exploiting redundancy in feature space (when n ≪ N), the same target reward can be realized with far fewer flips than a naive attack. Formulated as convex optimization (ℓ1 case → linear program) minimizing ‖ζ‖ s.t. Φζ = Φ(θ_A−θ_O) and box constraints keeping η ∈ [0,1].

## Proposed defense or method
This is primarily an attack/analysis paper, but it yields defense-relevant tools: tight lower and upper bounds (Theorems 2–3) on minimum poisoning cost, and Proposition 4 showing that a more expressive (higher-dimension n, richer row space) fixed feature extractor raises the attacker's required cost — i.e., representation capacity confers robustness to label flipping. The cost-minimization post-processor also serves as a robustness-evaluation instrument for defenders.

## Evaluation methodology
Theoretical: convex/linear-programming formulation with Lagrangian-dual bounds (lower bound Theorem 2, upper bound Theorem 3), feasibility/uniqueness analysis under fixed embedding, and an adaptive-embedding extension. Empirical: apply the post-processing to baseline label-flipping attacks and measure reduction in required flips vs baselines, especially as the ratio of feature dimension n to dataset size N varies.

## Metrics
Attack cost = number of label flips (ℓ1 norm of θ_A−θ_O) or a general norm; reported as reduction in flips relative to baseline attacks while preserving the same induced reward/policy. Bounds expressed via ‖(Φ†Φ)(θ_A−θ_O)‖ and its dual norm.

## Main findings
First theoretical characterization of minimum-cost label-flipping poisoning for RLHF/DPO. The problem is a convex program (LP for ℓ1); tight lower/upper cost bounds are derived. A model-agnostic post-processor can take any existing label-flipping attack and realize the same poisoning effect with equal-or-fewer flips (‖θ_A*−θ_O‖ ≤ ‖θ_A−θ_O‖). Cost reduction is significant when the reward model's feature dimension is small relative to dataset size (rank of Φ†Φ ≪ its dimension). More capable/higher-dimensional fixed feature extractors require higher attack cost (Proposition 4).

## Negative results
The optimization can be infeasible when the loss admits multiple minimizers yielding different optimal policies (addressed via a uniqueness result under a mild assumption / fixed embedding). Benefits shrink when n approaches N (little feature-space redundancy to exploit).

## Limitations stated by the authors
Explicit "Discrepancy Between Theory and Practice": the theory idealizes the attacker (continuous control of annotation probability η rather than binary flips) and the victim (minimizing population/expected loss rather than empirical loss). Realizable probabilities are discrete (multiples of 1/m for m annotations per triplet); authors acknowledge risk of deviation from practice, evaluated empirically.

## Design implications
RLHF/DPO reward learning has a quantifiable, often low, worst-case poisoning cost; alignment pipelines should treat preference-label integrity as a security-critical asset. Reward-model representation capacity (feature dimension) is a design knob that measurably increases poisoning cost.

## Implementation implications
Defenders should evaluate their pipelines with the cost-minimization tool to estimate how few untrusted annotations suffice to steer the model; consider higher-capacity fixed feature extractors, annotation redundancy (multiple annotators per triplet, larger m), trusted-annotator weighting, and label-anomaly / influence-based detection. Provenance and validation of preference labels are essential.

## Evaluation implications
Robustness to poisoning should be assessed against cost-optimized (not just naive) label-flipping adversaries; the ratio n/N is a key determinant of vulnerability and should be reported. Empirical results depend on discretization (m annotations), so evaluations must account for the theory-practice gap.

## Deployment implications
Allowing untrusted individuals to annotate preference data carries a bounded but potentially small cost to fully redirect the reward model; deployments relying on crowdsourced/outsourced preference labeling should assume this exposure and add data-validation and rollback controls.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Annotation provenance logging, per-annotator flip-rate monitoring, and detection of label patterns consistent with cost-minimized flips (e.g., concentrated in low-rank feature directions) support incident detection; the derived cost bounds provide a quantitative risk threshold to alert on.

## Applicability boundaries
Core to LLM/agent security: a training-time data-poisoning attack on the alignment (preference-optimization) pipeline that directly threatens safety policies. Findings apply to RLHF/DPO reward-model learning with linear reward heads over LLM embeddings. They do NOT directly transfer to inference-time attacks (jailbreak/prompt-injection), to non-preference supervised poisoning, or to settings where attackers can modify (x,y,z) rather than only labels; the tightest results assume fixed embedding and Assumption 1.
