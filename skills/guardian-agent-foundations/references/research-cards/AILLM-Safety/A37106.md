## Research question
Does the "first-token-centric" security picture of autoregressive (AR) LLMs still hold for diffusion LLMs (dLLMs),
and if not, where should safety alignment be concentrated to best defend dLLMs?

## Problem definition
AR-LLM safety research converges on controlling the first few response tokens ("Shallow Safety Alignment"), creating
a symmetric attacker/defender contest over initial tokens. dLLMs generate non-autoregressively (masked → iteratively
unmasked, bidirectional context), so it is unknown which token positions govern safety and whether the AR analysis
transfers. There is essentially no prior safety study of dLLMs.

## System or model being studied
dLLMs, illustrated with LLaDA-8B-Instruct ("Llada"). Proposed defense: MOSA (Middle-tOken Safety Alignment), a
reinforcement-learning alignment method that aligns middle-position generation (defined as tokens 20–60) with
predefined safe-refusal sentences that each end in an end-of-sentence "breaker" token, trained via a LoRA adapter with
a contrastive reward plus a KL penalty to a frozen reference model.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: jailbreak the dLLM into producing harmful/prohibited content.
- Adversary knowledge: black-box (submits crafted prompts; no weights). A white-box GCG adaptation is used by the
  authors as an analysis "upper bound," but the deployment threat model is black-box.
- Adversary access: unlimited crafted prompts via the model interface; prefilling of response positions used as an
  analysis probe.
- Adversary capabilities: manipulate prompt; attempt to force content at chosen positions (initial vs middle).
- Attacker budget: unlimited queries (stated); GCG run for 100 iterations in the probing experiment.
- Phase: inference (attack); training/fine-tuning (defense).
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted (specific harmful requests from AdvBench).
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: includes an adaptive analysis — GCG optimized to force middle-token content, and prompts
  that try to control middle positions — to test whether attackers can reach middle tokens.
- Explicitly out of scope: prompt injection and data leakage (authors restrict to jailbreak).

## Trust assumptions
Defender can fine-tune/align the model (LoRA). Assumes dLLMs exhibit a strong, input-independent sequential
generation bias (empirically shown: near-linear position-vs-step correlation for both benign Alpaca and adversarial
AdvBench prompts), which shields middle tokens from attacker control. Reference model is trusted for KL regularization.

## Attack or failure mechanism
Three analyses establish a security **asymmetry**: (1) Prefilling harmful/affirmative phrases at initial vs middle
(40/80/120/160th) positions — attack effectiveness *increases* the later the prefill position, so middle tokens
matter more for dLLM safety (GPT-4.1 1–5 harmfulness, >3 = jailbreak). (2) Optimization-based GCG forcing content at
the 1st vs 30th token: initial-token attack succeeds 33%, middle-token attack only 2%, because middle-token
optimization loss stays high — attackers cannot reliably reach the middle. (3) Generation-order analysis shows dLLMs
generate left-to-right in practice, the root cause of (2). Net: the attacker's influence is confined to the periphery
(early tokens); the defender can fortify the naturally shielded middle.

## Proposed defense or method
MOSA aligns tokens 20–60 with a positive set of safe-refusal sentences (each containing an EOS "breaker" that caps
harmful length even if early tokens are compromised) and penalizes a negative (harmful) set. For each step it scores
one positive and one negative sentence by the max log-likelihood over contiguous segments in the target window; the
contrastive reward is (positive − negative). A KL-divergence term against a frozen reference model preserves utility.
Only a LoRA adapter is updated. Objective: L(θ)=E[R(y|x) − β·D_KL(P_θ‖P_ref)].

## Datasets and benchmarks
Two standard jailbreak benchmarks (AdvBench used explicitly; harmfulness scored by GPT-4.1). Utility tested on coding,
math, and general reasoning. Alpaca used as a benign set for generation-order analysis. Compared against several
baselines and eight attack methods (per abstract).

## Evaluation methodology
Security compared vs multiple baselines across eight attack methods on two benchmarks; utility measured on
coding/math/general reasoning to check alignment tax; analysis experiments (prefilling, GCG position, generation order)
to justify the middle-token design.

## Metrics
Attack Success Rate; GPT-4.1 harmfulness score (1–5, jailbreak if >3); utility metrics on coding/math/reasoning;
optimization loss curves; average unmasked-token position per decoding step.

## Main findings
(1) For dLLMs, middle tokens (not initial) are most critical to output safety. (2) A capability asymmetry exists:
attackers can influence early tokens (33% GCG at token 1) but not middle tokens (2% at token 30), due to inherent
sequential generation bias. (3) MOSA, which aligns the shielded middle window, substantially reduces ASR while
preserving coding/math/reasoning utility (author claim: "strongly prove the superiority of MOSA"). Exact ASR/utility
numbers are truncated in the extract.

## Negative results
Middle-token adversarial optimization essentially fails (2% ASR) — a negative result *for the attacker*, which is the
paper's central lever. No reported failure mode of MOSA in the extracted text.

## Limitations stated by the authors
Not visible in the extracted text (discussion truncated). The design implicitly assumes the sequential-generation
bias holds.

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis)_
- Single model family (LLaDA-8B). Whether the middle-token asymmetry holds for other dLLMs (Dream, Gemini Diffusion,
  MMaDA) or future dLLMs with weaker sequential bias is untested — and the defense hinges entirely on that bias.
- The asymmetry could erode if a dLLM is trained/decoded to unmask non-sequentially, or if an attacker steers the
  *decoding order* rather than token content — not evaluated.
- Fixed middle window (20–60) and refusal templates may be gamed by attacks that push harmful content before token 20
  or after token 60; length "breaker" limits but does not eliminate harm.
- GPT-4.1-as-judge harmfulness scoring inherits judge bias.
- Explicitly excludes prompt injection and data leakage, so it is not a general dLLM-safety solution.

## Design implications
Architecture matters for where to place safety controls: defenses tuned for AR "first-token" alignment may be
suboptimal or misplaced for dLLMs. Exploit architecture-specific asymmetries — defend the positions the attacker
structurally cannot reach. An EOS "breaker" that bounds harmful output length is a cheap, transferable idea.

## Implementation implications
For dLLM-backed agents, safety alignment should target middle-generation windows via RL/LoRA, keep a frozen reference
for KL to protect utility, and combine refusal-content alignment with length capping. Verify the target dLLM's
generation-order bias before relying on the asymmetry.

## Evaluation implications
Safety benchmarks must be architecture-aware: report position-resolved robustness (prefill/optimization at multiple
positions), generation-order diagnostics, and the utility tax on coding/math/reasoning — not just a single ASR number.

## Deployment implications
dLLMs may offer a defender-favorable asymmetry today, but this is a property of current decoding behavior, not a
guarantee; monitor for decoding-order or windowing attacks. Frame within OWASP LLM jailbreak risk.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Instrument decoding to record per-step unmasked positions; anomalous non-sequential unmasking or content appearing in
the protected middle window is a tamper signal. Log harmfulness-judge scores per position.

## Applicability boundaries
**Core** agent-security relevance (jailbreak defense / safety alignment), but specific to diffusion LLMs. Findings
should NOT be generalized to AR-LLMs (where the first-token analysis still applies) or assumed stable for dLLMs whose
sequential bias differs. Silent on prompt injection, tool abuse, multimodal, and data leakage.

## Evidence strength

mechanism (sequential-generation bias), plus utility testing; but evidence rests on a single dLLM, the defense's core
assumption is that same bias, and end-to-end ASR/utility magnitudes are truncated in the extract.
