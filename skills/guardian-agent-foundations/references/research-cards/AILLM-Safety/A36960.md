## Research question
Can paralinguistic cues (emotion, intonation, speech rate, non-verbal sounds) be leveraged to detect toxic speech
whose toxicity originates outside textual content, and how can a benchmark and model be built to support this?

## Problem definition
Text-based content moderation misses toxicity carried by non-verbal/paralinguistic signals (e.g., sarcastic tone,
moaning/ASMR-like sexual cues) even when the transcript is benign. Existing public speech-toxicity datasets
(chiefly DeToxy-B) label toxicity only from text, and most paralinguistic-aware datasets are private with opaque
construction, blocking reproducible research.

## System or model being studied
(1) ToxiAlert-Bench: an English audio toxicity dataset — the paper reports 32,561 samples / 60.82 hours (abstract
says "over 30,000 clips"; intro says "over 60 hours"), 19,745 from established corpora + 12,816 synthesized,
7:1:2 train/val/test split. Annotations: 7 toxic categories (Sarcasm, Horror, Sexual, Mental & Risk, Ideology,
Violence & Harm, Discrimination) + Safe; 20 fine-grained labels; and a novel toxicity-**source** label
(textual-only 6,953; paralinguistic-only 6,728; both 2,551; safe 16,329). (2) ToxiAlert: a dual-head neural
network on a pre-trained SSL foundation model (wav2vec2.0-style) with two heads (source head: textual/paralinguistic;
type head: toxic category), trained with independent head training then joint fine-tuning, plus class-balanced
sampling and weighted loss.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: spread toxic/harmful audio content (harassment, sexual content, discrimination) on
  voice-based social platforms (live streaming, gaming, voice/video chat) while evading text-based moderation.
- Adversary knowledge: not formally modeled; implicitly gray/black-box w.r.t. the moderation system.
- Adversary access: ability to post/stream audio.
- Adversary capabilities: convey toxic intent through paralinguistic cues (tone, moaning, sarcasm) so the
  transcript stays benign — an evasion of text-only filters.
- Attacker budget: not stated in paper.
- Phase: deployment (content moderation at serving time).
- Targeted / untargeted: untargeted content moderation (per-clip classification), not an attack on a specific user.
- Digital / physical: digital audio (recorded/streamed/synthesized speech).
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: non-adaptive — no adversary that adapts to the detector is evaluated.

## Trust assumptions
Trusts the LMM/LLM annotation pipeline (Gemini-1.5-Flash, R1-AQA, GPT-4o) plus two human expert annotators
(Cohen's κ = 0.82). Assumes TTS-synthesized clips (GPT-4o text kept textually benign, DubbingX voices) faithfully
carry paralinguistic toxicity. Assumes SSL pre-trained representations transfer to toxicity discrimination.

## Attack or failure mechanism
Failure of incumbent defenses: text-based and text-dependent multi-task/feature-fusion models cannot detect
toxicity that is expressed only paralinguistically; benign semantics + harmful tone bypasses them. This is an
**evasion** of the moderation control, not an attack that corrupts training or the model.

## Proposed defense or method
A detection/content-moderation model (ToxiAlert). SSL foundation model produces representations; a dual-head
classifier separately predicts toxicity source and toxicity type; multi-stage training (per-head then joint
fine-tuning) reduces task interference; class-balanced sampling + weighted loss address imbalance.

## Datasets and benchmarks
- ToxiAlert-Bench (this paper's contribution). Bonafide sources: TIMIT, IEMOCAP, VCTK, LibriSpeech, VoxCeleb1,
  LJSpeech-1.1, CommonVoice, MELD. Synthetic: GPT-4o text + DubbingX TTS.
- Baseline comparison against DeToxy-B and SOTA multimodal LLM (MLLM) moderation solutions (e.g., commercial).

## Evaluation methodology
Benchmarking + ablations against academic baselines (DeToxy-B) and commercial MLLM-based moderation. Source-wise
breakdown (paralinguistic vs textual). Human hybrid verification of labels.

## Metrics
Macro-F1, accuracy, per-source accuracy/F1, Subset Accuracy. Reported: 21.1% relative Macro-F1 improvement and
13.0% relative accuracy gain over the strongest baseline; paralinguistic-source accuracy 91.18% / F1 83.30%;
textual-source accuracy 86.21% / F1 75.66%; Subset Accuracy improved 44.9% over the strongest baseline (author claims).

## Main findings
Leveraging paralinguistic features materially improves toxic-speech detection, especially for paralinguistic-only
toxicity that text-based methods miss; the dual-head design + multi-stage training outperforms academic and
commercial MLLM baselines under the evaluated conditions.

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis — not author claims)_
- Sample-count/duration figures are inconsistent between abstract ("over 30,000", "over 60 hours") and body (32,561
  / 60.82 h) — minor but worth verifying.
- ~39% of data is TTS-synthesized with GPT-4o + one TTS engine (DubbingX); paralinguistic toxicity may reflect that
  synthesis pipeline's stylistic priors rather than real-world abusive speech, risking distribution shift.
- Labels bootstrapped from LMMs/LLMs (Gemini, R1-AQA, GPT-4o) can inherit those models' biases; κ=0.82 covers human
  reviewers, not the automated stage.
- No adaptive adversary: a speaker who knows ToxiAlert exists could shift paralinguistic style to evade it — untested.
- English-only; toxicity taxonomy and paralinguistic cues are culture/language-specific.
- No evaluation of the model as a real-time moderation gate (latency, streaming, false-positive burden on benign
  emotional speech).

## Design implications
For agent/system safety stacks, treat audio/voice channels as a first-class moderation surface: text transcription +
LLM judgment alone can be evaded by paralinguistic toxicity. A source-aware classifier (is the harm textual or
tonal?) gives more actionable gate signals than a single toxic/benign bit.

## Implementation implications
Multi-head SSL classifiers can back an ActionGuard/PolicyGuard-style content filter for voice interfaces or
voice-enabled agents. Class-balanced sampling + weighted loss are needed because toxic classes are rare.

## Evaluation implications
Benchmarks for voice-agent safety should include paralinguistic-only and mixed-source toxic samples, not just
transcript-labeled toxicity; report per-source metrics and Subset Accuracy, and include commercial MLLM baselines.

## Deployment implications
A voice-moderation gate must weigh false positives on legitimately emotional/sarcastic speech; over-blocking is a
usability and fairness risk (not quantified here). Real-time/streaming constraints are unaddressed.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Emit toxicity-source and category as structured trace fields so downstream logging/audit can distinguish "benign
words, harmful tone" incidents. Retain audio provenance (bonafide vs synthetic) for forensic review.

## Applicability boundaries
This is a content-moderation / audio-toxicity-detection paper — **adjacent** to agent security, not core. Findings
generalize to voice-interface and voice-agent input moderation and to output moderation of TTS agents. They do NOT
provide guarantees against an adaptive adversary, non-English speech, or non-speech audio, and say nothing about
LLM jailbreak, tool abuse, or agent-to-agent threats.
