## Research question
How can a resource-light, graduate-level course raise baseline AI-safety awareness and evaluation competence in a Japanese science-and-engineering context, where policy momentum is strong but few courses explicitly connect ML/GenAI capabilities to systematic safety evaluation?

## Problem definition
This is an **education / curriculum-design case study**, not a technical security contribution. The authors define "AI safety education" as raising awareness and baseline competence in (i) recognizing common failure modes, (ii) planning simple evaluations, and (iii) proposing pragmatic mitigations — distinct from broader AI ethics. They observe a local gap: Japanese offerings tend to be ethics/literacy-forward with comparatively little structured technical safety practice (adversarial demos, evaluation planning, red-team exercises, model cards, incident post-mortems).

## System or model being studied
No model is trained or attacked. The "system" is a 7-week (compressed 14-week-equivalent) graduate elective at a private Japanese university: 35 students in 2024, 7 groups, weekly 3.5-hour blocks, no coding labs. Content progresses from ML foundations → generative models → alignment, with introductory agent topics (agent foundations, corrigibility, agent safety) taught conceptually. Delivery: lectures, invited talks, discussion studios, midterm team presentation, and a final paper-style report on risk evaluation and mitigation planning.

## Trust assumptions
Not applicable to a security system. Pedagogically the course assumes students arrive with high GenAI-tool exposure but limited safety vocabulary; mixed technical backgrounds (undergrad + grad, international exchange).

## Attack or failure mechanism
None demonstrated. Failure modes are described conceptually as learning targets (hallucination, memorization, distribution shift, prompt injection, jailbreaks, poisoning, reward misspecification, outer/inner misalignment).

## Proposed defense or method
The "method" is instructional design, built on three principles: (1) capabilities-first, safety-second sequencing; (2) conceptual (not computational) practice — analysis/reasoning/communication over programming; (3) "voices from practice" via invited industry/academic speakers. Assessment: attendance 20%, midterm presentation 30%, final presentation + paper-style report 50%.

## Datasets and benchmarks
No ML datasets. The empirical instrument is an anonymous end-of-course survey: N=35 enrolled, 19 valid responses (~54% response rate), five 5-point Likert items (3 learning-outcome + 2 experience-outcome) plus two open-ended prompts. A comparative Table 1 informally scans representative global (Stanford CS120, Princeton COS597Q, Berkeley DeCal, CMU 15-783, MIT, BlueDot) and Japanese (U. Tokyo, U. Tsukuba) offerings.

## Evaluation methodology
Single, voluntary, self-report questionnaire administered once at course end; light thematic coding of free-text. No pre/post quizzes, no rubric-rated blind assessment of capstone reports, no control group.

## Metrics
Self-reported Likert distributions. Reported: ~80% of respondents rated at least "Somewhat achieved" on ML fundamentals, application breadth, and safety reasoning; 84% satisfied; 79% described engagement as high; ~68.4% "Sufficiently achieved" on ML fundamentals. A ~10% "Not at all achieved" tail on knowledge items. Qualitative outcome: one group's capstone matured into a peer-reviewed ICLR 2025 workshop paper (anecdotal).

## Main findings
Author claim: a discussion-first, evaluation-centric, no-coding design is feasible and effective for raising AI-safety awareness and confidence in identifying risks and planning mitigations across a mixed-background cohort. The capabilities-first sequencing was credited by students for making safety concepts concrete.

## Negative results
Authors report a meaningful low-engagement / low-achievement tail attributed to (i) background heterogeneity, (ii) compressed-format cognitive load, and (iii) the deliberate no-coding constraint. Planned refinements: short pre-course primers/videos, 5–8-minute instructor-run micro-demos, structured think-pair-share and exit tickets, calibrated guest-lecture ratio.

## Limitations stated by the authors
Survey is voluntary, self-report, single-administration; may over-represent satisfied students; cannot attribute gains causally; no pre/post quizzes or blind rubric-rated capstone assessment. Single institution, single 2024 offering, small N.

## Design implications
For teams building an agent-security culture, the paper is a template for onboarding curricula: capability grounding → safety concepts → evaluation planning, with concrete artifacts (risk hypotheses, adversarial probes, red-team plans, model cards, incident post-mortems) as deliverables. It reinforces that "evaluation-centric" framing (plan a test for each claimed risk) is teachable without heavy tooling.

## Implementation implications
Minimal. If used to seed internal training, pair concept modules with the actual technical papers in this corpus (prompt injection, poisoning, jailbreak defenses) rather than relying on lecture-level abstraction.

## Evaluation implications
Underscores a governance-relevant gap: awareness is measured by self-report, not by demonstrated ability to design and run a safety evaluation. Any org adopting this model should add rubric-scored, blind-assessed capstones and pre/post competency checks.

## Deployment implications
None direct. Indirect: workforce readiness — a shared safety vocabulary among engineers reduces the chance that failure modes (jailbreak, injection, poisoning) go unrecognized in deployment reviews.

## Monitoring and incident implications
The curriculum names "incident post-mortems" and "monitoring artifacts" as desirable outputs but does not teach or evaluate them in depth; treat as an aspiration, not a validated practice.
