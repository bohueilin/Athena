## Research question
How can we holistically evaluate LLMs that Simulate Professions (SP-LLMs) as teachers along two intertwined axes — pedagogical/role-playing fidelity and adversarial safety (including education-specific harms like academic misconduct) — which existing benchmarks fail to measure?

## Problem definition
Foundational benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K) test knowledge/reasoning without a professional persona; scenario benchmarks (EduBench) prioritize task completion over end-to-end pedagogical fidelity; general safety benchmarks (SafetyBench) miss domain-specific ethical challenges (e.g., students requesting help with academic misconduct). This leaves no tool to jointly assess professional competence, role-playing fidelity, and ethical safety of Teacher SP-LLMs.

## System or model being studied
14 leading LLMs evaluated as simulated teachers (SP-LLMs). The artifact under study is the benchmark itself: EduGuardBench — a dual-component evaluation harness with a bilingual (English/Chinese) dataset built via a Human-in-the-Loop pipeline.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: induce the teacher-persona LLM to (a) produce pedagogically harmful responses (Incompetence, Indolence, Offensiveness) or (b) fulfill harmful/unethical requests, especially academic misconduct (ghost-writing, data fabrication, real-time exam cheating, plagiarism/misattribution) and general harms.
- Adversary knowledge: black-box, prompt-level (persona-based jailbreak).
- Adversary access: supplies a paired Teacher Prompt (assigns a professional identity + a flawed core belief) and a Student Request (harmful intent disguised as a plausible educational question).
- Adversary capabilities: persona role-play jailbreak that forces trade-offs between the assigned persona and safety principles; disguises dangerous/illegal/unethical intent as benign inquiry across four domains (arts/psychological, health risk, technology/security, political/ideological) plus academic misconduct.
- Attacker budget: 801 curated open-ended adversarial prompts; no optimization/gradients.
- Phase: inference / deployment; evaluation-time.
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted (specific harmful or cheating outcome).
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: fixed curated prompts (non-adaptive), human-reviewed for subtlety.

## Trust assumptions
Assumes a benign deploying platform but an adversarial (or naive) student user, and that assigning a plausible authoritative persona with a flawed belief can pressure the model to override safety. Evaluation trusts expert human annotators plus an LLM-based ASR judge.

## Attack or failure mechanism
Two failure surfaces. (1) Pedagogical harm: on Select-All-That-Apply (SATA) teaching questions, the model may select distractors reflecting Incompetence (factual error), Indolence (dismissiveness), or Offensiveness (condescension). (2) Adversarial safety: persona-based jailbreak — a Teacher Prompt establishes identity + a well-intentioned-but-flawed belief that "justifies" fulfilling the student's harmful request, eliciting dangerous/unethical content or academic-misconduct assistance. Related mechanisms discussed: adversarial suffixes (Zou et al. 2023), single-point dependency in multi-step instruction chains, and knowledge-injection attacks.

## Proposed defense or method
No defense is proposed; the contribution is the benchmark + evaluation protocol. It introduces the concept of an "Educational Refusal" (converting a harmful request into a teachable moment) as a positive safety behavior and a three-tier Refusal Quality assessment, providing measurement rather than mitigation.

## Datasets and benchmarks
EduGuardBench, two components: (I) Teaching Harm — SATA question set of 2636 entries across five student-oriented scenarios (Problem Solving, Error Correction, Idea Provision, Personalized Learning Support, Emotional Support), embedding S1/S2/S3 ethical-flaw distractors; incorporates 100 authentic questions (textbooks, exams, GAOKAO). (II) Adversarial Safety — 801 open-ended persona-based jailbreak prompts over four harm domains + academic misconduct. Bilingual (English/Chinese) with translation/back-translation verification. Built via 5-stage HITL pipeline (seed → LLM expansion → automated pre-screening → iterative HITL review → bilingual verification).

## Evaluation methodology
Dual protocol. Teaching Harm: score each SATA item as Perfect Match (1.0), Omission (0.5), or Incorrect Inclusion (0.0); aggregate into Role-playing Fidelity Score (RFS) plus Accuracy/Omission/Inclusion rates and an ethical-flaw distribution (S1/S2/S3). Adversarial Safety: open-ended responses manually evaluated with an Attack Success Rate (a response "substantively aids" the harmful/cheating request) and a three-tier Refusal Quality assessment (including Educational Refusal). Experiments over 14 SOTA models.

## Metrics
- Role-playing Fidelity Score (RFS); Accuracy (Acc), Omission Rate (Omit), Inclusion Rate (Incl); per-scenario error rate (Err%) and S1/S2/S3 distribution.
- Attack Success Rate (ASR) — primary safety metric.
- Refusal Quality (three-tier), including Educational Refusal.

## Main findings
- Author claim: stark polarization across models; reasoning-oriented models generally show superior fidelity, but Incompetence is the dominant failure mode across most models.
- Author claim: a counter-intuitive "scaling paradox" — mid-sized models can be the most vulnerable, challenging monotonic "bigger = safer" assumptions.
- Author claim: an "Educational Transformation Effect" — the safest models excel at converting harmful requests into teachable moments (ideal Educational Refusals), a capacity strongly negatively correlated with ASR (proposed as a new dimension of advanced AI safety).
- Author claim: Emotional Support scenarios challenge all models the most.

## Negative results
- Even leading models exhibit non-trivial pedagogical harm (Incompetence prevalent) and are manipulable via persona jailbreaks; larger scale does not guarantee lower ASR (scaling paradox).

## Limitations stated by the authors
Evaluation limited to English and Chinese (cross-cultural validation needed); relatively small sample for paired analysis limits statistical power; synthetic data generation may not capture authentic student-teacher interaction complexity; text-only (needs multimodal extension); expert annotation introduces subjectivity (automated/objective metrics could improve scalability); findings lack validation in real educational settings (deployment studies needed).

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis — clearly not author claims)_
- ASR relies partly on manual evaluation and (per related-work framing) LLM-assisted judging; inter-annotator agreement/judge-reliability numbers are not in the extracted text.
- The 801 adversarial prompts are a fixed, non-adaptive set; models could overfit or benchmark contamination could occur once released publicly.
- "Educational Refusal" as a safety-positive is a normative construct defined by the authors; its correlation with lower ASR is observational and could reflect general capability rather than a distinct safety mechanism.
- Persona-jailbreak taxonomy and model list are partly in appendices (truncated here), limiting full independent scoring reproduction.

## Design implications
Educational/professional-persona assistants need domain-specific safety design: refusing academic-misconduct assistance and pedagogically harmful outputs, not just general-harm refusal. The Educational Transformation Effect suggests designing for *constructive refusals* (redirect to learning) rather than bare refusals, and that persona assignment is itself an attack surface requiring guardrails.

## Implementation implications
Guardrails for SP-LLMs should detect persona-plus-flawed-belief prompt patterns and academic-misconduct intents, and reward "teachable-moment" reframing. Because mid-sized models can be most vulnerable, safety hardening cannot rely on scale alone; explicit refusal-quality tuning is indicated.

## Evaluation implications
Safety benchmarks for role-playing/professional LLMs should jointly measure fidelity and adversarial safety, include domain-specific harms (e.g., academic misconduct), and score refusal *quality* (not just refusal vs. comply). ASR alone under-measures advanced safety; Educational Refusal is a proposed additional axis. Guard against benchmark contamination after public release.

## Deployment implications
Deploying LLMs as tutors requires special protection for emotionally sensitive tasks (weakest scenario), domain-specific misconduct policies, and per-model (not per-size) safety validation. Real-setting deployment studies are still needed before trust.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Monitor for persona-based jailbreak prompts (teacher identity + flawed justification) and student academic-misconduct requests; track ASR and refusal-quality distributions in production. A shift toward bare (non-educational) refusals or fulfilled misconduct requests is an incident signal.

## Applicability boundaries
Core to LLM/agent safety — specifically evaluation of persona/role-play jailbreak resistance and domain-specific (educational) harms. Findings apply to text-based, English/Chinese teacher-SP-LLM settings on the tested 14 models; do NOT generalize the scaling paradox or absolute ASR to other domains, languages, multimodal settings, or to real classroom deployments (authors explicitly caution). The benchmark measures but does not mitigate.

Tempered by: non-adaptive fixed prompt set, partial reliance on subjective/expert (and possibly LLM) grading without reported agreement, small paired-analysis sample, and no real-deployment validation.
