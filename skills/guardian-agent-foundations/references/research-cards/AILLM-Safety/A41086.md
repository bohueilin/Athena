## Research question
State-of-the-art diffusion models now render legible text inside images; can they be induced to embed NSFW/offensive text (insults, slurs, explicit terms), do existing visual/language NSFW mitigations address it, and how can the model itself be made safe against this without harming benign text/image quality?

## Problem definition
Prior NSFW work targets visual scenes (violence/nudity). A novel, under-addressed threat is offensive text embedded within generated images. Visual-domain and language-domain mitigations either fail to suppress harmful embedded text or severely degrade benign text generation; input/output filtering is context-blind, OCR-fragile, and inapplicable to open-weight/locally deployed models.

## System or model being studied
Text-to-image diffusion models: SD3, SDXL, FLUX, DeepFloyd IF (and SD1.4 for ESD compatibility). Text is rendered by specific attention layers (joint attention in SD3, cross-attention in SDXL/DeepFloyd IF), identified via prior parameter-localization work (Staniszewski et al. 2025). Baselines adapted/evaluated: AURA (neuron dampening), ESD (concept erasure), Safe-CLIP (CLIP-encoder fine-tuning), plus naive prompt-rewriting and OCR+Detoxify pipelines.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: cause a T2I model to render NSFW/offensive text (slurs, explicit terms, potentially hate speech/propaganda) in output images.
- Adversary knowledge: includes white-box/open-weight settings where users run the model locally and can bypass API-side filtering.
- Adversary access: prompt input; for open models, full model access.
- Adversary capabilities: prompts that trigger embedded NSFW text; exploits context-dependence (benign-looking words become offensive in visual context) and OCR/detector fragility (spelling artifacts evade text toxicity classifiers).
- Attacker budget: low; standard prompting.
- Phase: inference / deployment; mitigation operates at fine-tuning (training) time.
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted (specific offensive terms) but also inadvertent generation.
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: primarily demonstrates the threat and a mitigation; not an adaptive attacker study.

## Trust assumptions
Assumes the text-rendering layers are localizable and that fine-tuning only those layers preserves general capability; assumes paired NSFW/benign image supervision (differing by one embedded word) teaches generalizable suppression; assumes human judgment as ground truth for "offensive" where detectors fail.

## Attack or failure mechanism
Modern DMs integrate multiple text encoders (CLIP/T5) enabling legible in-image text; this capability also renders NSFW text. Off-the-shelf toxicity detectors miss context-dependent terms (e.g., "Penetrating", "Cocks") and are defeated by minor spelling errors/artifacts; visual NSFW detectors (Multiheaded Detector, SD Filter) and even OCR+Detoxify leave large fractions undetected (e.g., FLUX OCR+Detoxify detects only ~91%, SDXL <50%). Filtering is also bypassable on open/local models.

## Proposed defense or method
Lightweight LoRA fine-tuning applied only to localized text-generation attention layers. Supervision: a curated safety fine-tuning dataset of NSFW/benign prompt pairs differing by a single word, with image pairs identical except the embedded word; the model is conditioned on the NSFW prompt but trained to produce the benign image. This modifies weights directly (works white-box/open-weight) and is intended to generalize suppression to unseen NSFW terms while preserving benign text and overall image quality.

## Datasets and benchmarks
ToxicBench (new, open-source; built on CreativeBench): curated NSFW-triggering prompts, the safety fine-tuning dataset, new text/image-quality metrics, and an evaluation pipeline assessing NSFW-ness plus text and image quality. Detection comparison uses Multiheaded Detector (Qu et al. 2023), SD Filter (Rando et al. 2022), and a custom OCR+Detoxify pipeline.

## Evaluation methodology
Measure change in embedded-text generation on NSFW vs benign words (want high change on NSFW, low on benign) using the proposed N-gram Levenshtein Distance (NGramLD); assess image quality preservation; compare against AURA, ESD (on SD1.4), Safe-CLIP, and naive filtering baselines; quantify detector failure rates (Table 1).

## Metrics
NGramLD (character-level deleted/added/substituted count of rendered words after intervention — high for NSFW = good, low for benign = good); detection success rate (MHD, SD Filter, OCR+Detoxify percentages); image-quality metrics (FID/MMD-family; full details in appendix).

## Main findings
All four SOTA DMs readily embed NSFW text. Existing detectors are insufficient (OCR+Detoxify best but still misses ~9% on FLUX, <50% detection on SDXL; visual detectors worse). Baseline mitigations (AURA, Safe-CLIP) degrade benign text nearly as much as NSFW text (e.g., AURA NSFW NGramLD +2.56 vs benign +2.20; Safe-CLIP +2.87 vs +2.65) — i.e., they cause overall quality loss rather than targeted suppression; ESD on SD1.4 gives the best baseline trade-off (benign +2.10 vs NSFW +3.30). The proposed layer-localized LoRA method aims for targeted NSFW suppression while preserving benign generations and is applicable in white-box/open-weight settings unlike filtering.

## Negative results
Naive prompt-preprocessing and OCR-censoring both fail: context-blind, latency-inducing, and inapplicable to open models; OCR-based detection breaks under spelling artifacts; visual NSFW detectors are not designed for embedded text (SD Filter's partial success is an incidental CLIP effect). ESD is incompatible with SD3's flow-matching (fixed noise schedule), limiting some baselines.

## Limitations stated by the authors
Framed as an initial step; ESD cannot be applied to SD3; baseline methods trade off benign quality; full setup/ablations deferred to appendices. Broader limitations of the proposed method's generalization are acknowledged as future work.

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis)_ "Offensive" ground truth relies partly on human judgment where detectors disagree, introducing subjectivity; generalization to unseen NSFW terms and to non-English scripts is asserted but bounded by the fine-tuning vocabulary; layer-localization depends on prior work that may not transfer to future architectures; publishing ToxicBench and NSFW-triggering prompts is dual-use; the method requires weight access (does not help pure API consumers who cannot fine-tune).

## Design implications
NSFW safety for multimodal generators must cover embedded-text rendering, not only scene content; interventions should be localized to the responsible parameters (text-generation layers) to avoid collateral benign degradation. Weight-level mitigation is necessary for open-weight models where deployment-time filtering can be bypassed.

## Implementation implications
Prefer targeted LoRA on text-rendering layers over global fine-tuning or encoder-wide edits; pair-based supervision (single-word swap) localizes the safety signal. Input/output filters should be treated as insufficient alone (context-blindness, OCR fragility) and combined with model-level mitigation.

## Evaluation implications
Report both NSFW-suppression and benign-preservation (a good mitigation maximizes the gap, e.g., high NSFW NGramLD, low benign NGramLD) plus image quality; detector-only evaluation understates the threat because detectors miss context-dependent and artifact-obscured offensive text.

## Deployment implications
Open-weight T2I deployment cannot rely on API-side filtering; model weights themselves must be hardened. Providers should assume embedded-text NSFW is a live, largely undetected risk and evaluate models with ToxicBench before release.

## Monitoring and incident implications
OCR+toxicity monitoring will systematically under-report embedded offensive text; monitoring should include image-space and human-in-the-loop review; NGramLD-style metrics can track regression after model/policy updates.

## Applicability boundaries
Adjacent to agent security: a multimodal generative-safety threat and a weight-level mitigation, transferable to content-moderation and safe-model-release practice. It does NOT concern agentic tool use, prompt-injection into agents, credential handling, or autonomous action authorization. Relevant to Origin/Passport mainly as evidence that deployment-time filters are bypassable on open weights and that safety must be built into the artifact and verified with benchmarks.
