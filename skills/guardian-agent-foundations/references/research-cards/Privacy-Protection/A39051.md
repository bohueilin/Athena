## Research question
Can the data producing *all three* components of a linear program — cost c, constraint matrix A, and constraint vector b — be simultaneously privatized with differential privacy while *guaranteeing* that the solution of the privatized LP still satisfies the original, non-private constraints (feasibility), and with quantifiably bounded loss of optimality?

## Problem definition
LPs of the form maximize c(D)ᵀx s.t. A(D)x ≤ b(D), x ≥ 0 are formulated from a sensitive database D. Publishing the optimal x* leaks information about D. Prior DP-LP work either violated original constraints (Hsu 2014b; Cummings 2015; Kaplan 2024) or privatized only some terms (Munoz 2021 privatized b; Benvenuti 2024a privatized A). Munoz et al. 2021 posed as an open problem the simultaneous privatization of the constraint-generating data with guaranteed feasibility. This paper solves it and extends privatization to A, b, and c together.

## System or model being studied
A privacy pipeline (Algorithm 1) that:
- Privatizes A(D) with a new **Matrix-Variate Truncated Laplace Mechanism** (Lemma 3.2), adding sₐ·1 + truncated-Laplace noise only to structurally non-zero entries (preserving "non-sensitive" structural zeros), such that coefficients can only grow — tightening constraints.
- Privatizes b(D) with the multivariate **Truncated Laplace Mechanism** (Munoz 2021; Geng 2020), subtracting s_b·1 so each constraint only tightens.
- Privatizes c(D) with the standard (unbounded) **Laplace Mechanism** (cost does not affect feasibility).
- Composes these via sequential composition (Lemma 2.7) with budget split α_A, α_b, α_c; post-processing immunity (Lemma 2.8) means solving the private LP does not weaken privacy.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: infer entries of the sensitive database D from released LP artifacts / solution.
- Adversary knowledge: DP worst-case — bounds hold for any adversary regardless of side information; publicly known are the data bounds (Assumption 2.4) and problem structure.
- Adversary access: observes the privatized problem and/or its solution (and arbitrary post-processing thereof).
- Adversary capabilities: unbounded computation; DP guarantee is information-theoretic under (ε, δ).
- Attacker budget: N/A (worst-case DP).
- Phase: deployment / release time (privatize at problem-formulation, release solution).
- Targeted / untargeted: protects any single differing database entry (adjacency = differ in ≤1 entry, Definition 2.5).
- Digital / physical: digital (though motivated by power-grid, transportation, finance data).
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: DP is adaptive-robust by construction; no explicit adaptive-attack experiment.

## Trust assumptions
Data bounds on D are public (Assumption 2.4). Slater's condition holds for every realization (Assumption 2.2). A common feasible point exists across all database realizations (Assumption 3.6, "Perturbed Feasibility"). The mechanism operator (who forms and privatizes the LP) is trusted (central-DP model); the released solution and any downstream computation inherit the DP guarantee.

## Attack or failure mechanism
Leakage channel: the optimizer x* is a deterministic function of A(D), b(D), c(D); publishing it reveals information about D. Naïve DP with unbounded (Gaussian/Laplace) noise can perturb constraints arbitrarily, potentially making the private solution **infeasible** with respect to the original constraints — unacceptable when constraints encode safety. The technical failure being fixed is thus "privacy that breaks feasibility."

## Proposed defense or method
Differential privacy via *asymmetric, one-sided-tightening* truncated-Laplace noise on A and b so the private feasible region is a subset of the original, plus standard Laplace on c. Formal results: (Thm 3.9) the composed mechanism is (ε, δ)-DP; (Thm 3.10) the private solution is feasible for the original non-private constraints (solves the open problem); (Thm 4.1) analytic bound on expected sub-optimality in terms of ε, δ, giving a privacy–accuracy calibration tool. Structural zeros in A and non-sensitive zeros in c are preserved.

## Datasets and benchmarks
Synthetic/numerical LPs. Coefficients pᵢⱼ(D) drawn as: 0 w.p. 0.2, Uniform[0,1] w.p. 0.8; b_i = 10⁷, n_j = 10⁷; M (number of decision variables/constraints) swept over {5,…,100} with 100 samples each; ε ∈ [0.25, 2], δ = 0.1. No real-world dataset; motivating domains cited (portfolio/Markowitz, power systems, transportation).

## Evaluation methodology
Numerical simulation comparing sub-optimality and constraint-violation rate against (Hsu et al. 2014b) and (Munoz et al. 2021). Case (i): privatize A and c (vs Hsu). Case (ii): privatize A, b, and c (vs Munoz, which can only keep b private). Scalability studied by increasing M.

## Metrics
- Constraint-violation rate (fraction of original constraints violated by the private solution).
- Sub-optimality (increase in optimal cost relative to state of the art).
- Computation/scalability trend vs M.
- Headline: 65% reduction in sub-optimality vs SOTA under a "typical privacy setup"; zero constraint violation (Thm 3.10) vs up to 51% violation for Hsu 2014b at ε = 0.25; ~10% sub-optimality increase for a 10× increase in M; at most 6% performance difference between case (i) and (ii); ~20% sub-optimality increase vs Munoz at ε = 2 (cost of also privatizing A).

## Main findings
Author claims: first method to simultaneously privatize A, b, c with guaranteed original-constraint satisfaction; always produces a feasible solution (Thm 3.10); 65% sub-optimality reduction vs SOTA; scalable (privacy adds no computational-complexity penalty). Solves the Munoz et al. 2021 open problem.

## Negative results
Privatizing A in addition to b/c costs accuracy (~20% higher sub-optimality vs Munoz at ε = 2) — an explicit privacy–utility trade-off. Requires Slater's condition and a common feasible point; if the feasible region has empty interior, privacy and feasibility are "fundamentally incompatible" (Remark 2.3).

## Limitations stated by the authors
Method is for *linear* programs with *deterministic linear* constraints; future work targets nonlinear and stochastic constraints. Requires publicly known data bounds and the perturbed-feasibility assumption.

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis — not author claims)_
- Central-DP trust model: a single trusted curator forms and privatizes the LP; no local-DP or untrusted-aggregator variant.
- One-sided constraint tightening shrinks the feasible region — in tightly constrained real systems this can materially reduce achievable objective or (if Assumption 3.6 fails) make the private problem's guarantees inapplicable.
- Evaluation is purely synthetic; no real portfolio/power-grid dataset validates the sensitivity/accuracy trade-off in practice.
- (ε, δ) accounting is per-release; repeated re-solves on the same D consume budget (composition), not discussed for streaming/online use.
- No empirical privacy-attack (e.g., reconstruction) is run — reliance is on the formal DP guarantee, which is appropriate but leaves practical leakage of the *solution structure* unquantified.

## Design implications
When DP must coexist with hard safety constraints, prefer *bounded, direction-controlled* noise (truncated Laplace tightening) over unbounded Gaussian/Laplace so private solutions stay inside the true feasible set. Preserve known structural zeros to avoid injecting spurious couplings and wasting privacy budget. Budget-splitting across A/b/c lets designers prioritize which sensitive terms matter most.

## Implementation implications
The pipeline is a drop-in pre-processing wrapper around any LP solver (post-processing immunity). Sensitivity terms (Δ₁,₁A, Δ₁b, Δ₁c) and public data bounds must be computed up front; the analytic sub-optimality bound (Thm 4.1) can be exposed as a calibration knob to end users.

## Evaluation implications
DP mechanisms for constrained optimization should always report *both* privacy (ε, δ) and constraint-violation rate — feasibility is the property naïve DP silently breaks. Sweep ε and problem size to expose the accuracy–privacy–scale surface.

## Deployment implications
Applicable to privacy-preserving resource allocation, power-system rescheduling, portfolio and transportation optimization where constraint satisfaction is safety-critical and input data is sensitive. Sponsored by AFRL/ONR/AFOSR — a defense/controls context. Not a machine-learning training defense; it protects data that *parameterizes* an optimization.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Track cumulative privacy-budget expenditure across releases (each solve leaks ε, δ). Log the chosen (ε, δ) and the analytic sub-optimality bound per released solution as evidence for compliance/audit. There is no runtime "attack" to detect; the control is the mechanism itself.

## Applicability boundaries
Results hold for LPs with linear deterministic constraints satisfying Slater's condition and a common feasible point, under central DP with public data bounds. They should NOT be generalized to nonlinear/stochastic constraints, local-DP/untrusted-curator settings, ML model training, or LLM/agent privacy without new analysis. Relevance to agent security is *adjacent*: differential privacy is a general privacy primitive, but this paper's subject is optimization, not agents/tools/prompts.
