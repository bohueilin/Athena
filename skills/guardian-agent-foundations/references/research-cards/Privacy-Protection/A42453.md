## Research question
Can realistic, high-resolution face images be reconstructed from leaked face embeddings — including embeddings from privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems and protected/partial embeddings — well enough to impersonate identities against real-world and commercial face-recognition (FR) systems?

## Problem definition
FR systems store face embeddings/templates as biometric data. PPFR systems and template-protection schemes (hash-, HE-, transform-based) aim to protect these, but most PPFR conceals input-image visual info while embeddings themselves stay under-protected, and prior reconstruction attacks (a) mostly target normal FR (not PPFR), (b) produce blurry/noisy faces, or (c) are resource-intensive/slow. The paper asks how realistically embeddings can be inverted to faces, and uses that as a privacy-risk assessment tool.

## System or model being studied
FEM (Face Embedding Mapping): a framework that maps an arbitrary target FR/PPFR embedding into the embedding space of a pre-trained Identity-Preserving diffusion model (IPA-FaceID / IP-Adapter-FaceID), which then generates a realistic face. Two variants: FEM-MLP (fixed activations) and FEM-KAN (Kolmogorov-Arnold Network, learnable edge activations for non-linear mapping). Trained with MSE reconstruction loss between mapped and target-domain embeddings; ~3 layers each; trained on 90% of FFHQ, 20 epochs, Tesla V100.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: reconstruct a complete, realistic face image from a leaked embedding and use it to impersonate the victim (pass verification) against the same or a different FR system.
- Adversary knowledge (white/gray/black-box): **black-box** — attacker only obtains features/embeddings from the target feature extractor (query image → embedding), no internal weights; no access to the target's private training data.
- Adversary access: possesses a leaked face embedding of a registered user (from the FR/PPFR database); can inject reconstructed images into target FR systems (query/verification).
- Adversary capabilities: collect a public face dataset (e.g., FFHQ) or synthesize faces to train the FEM mapping; generate five candidate images per embedding.
- Attacker budget: modest — few-layer FEM, 20 epochs on a single V100; reconstruction is direct (fast) at inference, unlike training-heavy/slow prior methods.
- Phase (training / inference / deployment): inference/deployment (attack on deployed FR/PPFR + leaked template store).
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted (identity impersonation).
- Digital / physical: digital (injected images/embeddings); reconstructed faces are realistic enough to fool a commercial API.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: attack is non-adaptive to defenses (mapping learned generically), but is explicitly tested against multiple protection mechanisms.

## Trust assumptions
Assumes embeddings can leak (compromised template database / model serving). Assumes a mapping exists between same-identity embeddings from different backbones (target model vs IPA-FaceID's default FR), enabling cross-space mapping. Attacker cannot see private training data but can gather public data and query the target extractor black-box.

## Attack or failure mechanism
Model-inversion / template-inversion: FEM learns to translate a target FR/PPFR embedding into the IPA-FaceID embedding domain (bridging different backbones); the ID-preserving diffusion model then synthesizes a realistic same-identity face. KAN's univariate-function decomposition (Kolmogorov-Arnold theorem) captures the non-linear cross-embedding relationship better than MLP. Reconstructed faces are injected to real-world FR systems for transferable verification and to the commercial Face++ API — demonstrating that even PPFR/protected/partial embeddings retain enough identity information to invert.

## Proposed defense or method
This is an **attack** paper (with the stated dual purpose of being an evaluation tool for FR/PPFR privacy). No new defense is proposed; instead it stress-tests existing protections: PPFR models DCTDP, HFCF, PartialFace, MinusFace; embedding-protection transforms PolyProtect, MLP-Hash, SlerpFace; and the image-space privacy method Fawkes — showing they remain vulnerable to realistic reconstruction under the tested threat model.

## Datasets and benchmarks
Training: FFHQ (90% for FEM). Testing/generalization: CelebA-HQ (1000 never-before-seen identities), LADN (makeup/de-makeup impact). Target models to invert: normal FR — IRSE50, IR152; PPFR — DCTDP, HFCF, PartialFace, MinusFace. Real-world evaluation FR systems: ElasticFace (EF), MobileFace (MF), GhostFaceNet (GF), ArcFace (AF), plus commercial Face++ API. Baselines: FaceTI, MAP2V, and "None" (raw IPA-FaceID).

## Evaluation methodology
Three axes: (1) effectiveness — reconstruct 5 images per embedding, inject into four test FR systems, measure transferable verification success; (2) generalization — train on FFHQ, test on unseen CelebA-HQ identities; study markup/makeup effect on LADN; (3) robustness — reconstruct from partial embeddings and protected embeddings (PolyProtect, MLP-Hash, SlerpFace) and from Fawkes-protected faces. ASR computed at a fixed False Acceptance Rate (FAR) of 0.01 per FR model. UMAP visualizes embedding distributions before/after FEM mapping.

## Metrics
Attack Success Rate (ASR) = fraction of generated faces recognized as the target identity by real-world FR systems, at FAR=0.01. Selected reported averages (target → avg ASR over MF/EF/GF/AF): IRSE50 FEM-KAN 83.7 vs MAP2V 77.9 vs FaceTI 72.7 vs None 7.5; IR152 FEM-KAN 77.2 vs MAP2V 73.8; PPFR DCTDP FEM-KAN 84.4 (MAP2V 78.3); HFCF FEM-KAN 83.1 vs MAP2V 28.0 (large gap); MinusFace FEM-KAN avg with MAP2V collapsing (e.g., MAP2V 68.0/4.8/2.3/5.6 vs FEM-KAN 96.5/71.3/44.5/58.1). FEM-KAN generally ≥ FEM-MLP. Face++ confidence scores shown in figures (e.g., ~73–92 range).

## Main findings
Author claims: (1) FEM reconstructs realistic high-resolution faces from FR and PPFR embeddings, outperforming FaceTI and MAP2V in ASR across most targets; (2) FEM-KAN's learnable non-linear mapping beats FEM-MLP; (3) the attack is robust to partial and protected embeddings (PolyProtect, MLP-Hash, SlerpFace) and to Fawkes-protected faces, and reconstructed faces transfer to unseen real-world FR systems and bypass the commercial Face++ API — i.e., current PPFR/embedding protections remain vulnerable to template inversion; (4) FEM can serve as a privacy-leakage evaluation tool for FR/PPFR systems.

## Negative results
Against the strongest PPFR (MinusFace) and on harder evaluators (GhostFaceNet), absolute ASR is lower (e.g., FEM-KAN 44.5 on GF for MinusFace), showing protection reduces but does not eliminate reconstructability. Prior baselines (MAP2V) collapse on some protected settings (HFCF, MinusFace) where FEM still succeeds — a negative result for those baselines, a positive for the attack.

## Design implications
Demonstrates that face embeddings — even "protected" or partial ones — are effectively identity-bearing secrets: transform-based/PPFR protections and image-space methods (Fawkes) do not prevent realistic inversion under black-box access. Embedding stores must be treated as high-sensitivity credentials, not opaque hashes. Diffusion + KAN mapping raises the realism/transferability bar for inversion attacks.

## Implementation implications
For FR/PPFR builders: embedding-only protection is insufficient; consider defenses that break cross-backbone mappability, add liveness/presentation-attack detection at the query interface, and avoid persisting invertible templates. For red teams: a lightweight few-layer mapper into an ID-preserving diffusion space is a strong, low-cost inversion primitive.

## Evaluation implications
FEM is proposed as a standardized privacy-leakage evaluation tool: measure ASR of reconstructed faces (at fixed FAR, across multiple unseen evaluator FR systems and a commercial API) to audit whether an FR/PPFR scheme actually resists template inversion — a stronger bar than intra-system recognition-accuracy claims.

## Deployment implications
Deployed FR/PPFR systems that leak or expose embeddings are subject to realistic impersonation; template-protection marketing ("privacy-preserving") should be validated against inversion attacks like this before trust. Query-time defenses (liveness, injection detection) become essential because reconstructed faces are realistic enough to pass commercial verification.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Embedding-database exposure should be treated as an identity-compromise incident (equivalent to credential leak), triggering re-enrollment/revocation. Monitoring for injected/synthetic query images (anti-spoofing, presentation-attack detection) is the practical detection layer, since the reconstruction step is offline and undetectable server-side.
