## Research question
Can a classical verifier (without quantum hardware) obtain a non-interactive, succinct proof that a cloud-hosted quantum machine-learning (QML) inference was computed correctly, while the model's internal quantum parameters remain hidden?

## Problem definition
QML inference is pushed to scarce, costly cloud quantum hardware, creating tension between verifiability (can the client trust the result?) and confidentiality (are the model parameters protected?). Prior verification approaches need quantum-capable verifiers or interactive protocols (poor scalability); classical ZKML gives non-interactive proofs but doesn't cover quantum computation and struggles to scale. zkQML bridges ZKML and QML.

## System or model being studied
zkQML: a framework that converts parametrized quantum circuit (PQC) operations into classical prime-field arithmetic circuits that approximate the quantum computation, then generates zero-knowledge proofs over them. Pipeline stages: (1) PQC produces an inference; (2) arithmetic modeling maps input/gates/output into prime-field vectors + arithmetic circuit; (3) ZK backend compiles constraints and produces a classical ZK proof hiding parameters; (4) classical verifier validates non-interactively.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective (integrity side): an untrusted cloud quantum host returns an incorrect/forged inference result that the client cannot detect. Confidentiality side: a verifier/observer tries to learn the model's secret internal quantum parameters.
- Adversary knowledge (white/gray/black-box): verifier is classical and has no quantum capability; treats the quantum computation as opaque.
- Adversary access: remote, cloud-hosted quantum inference in an "untrusted environment" (author's phrasing).
- Adversary capabilities: could produce wrong results (verified against the proof) and/or attempt to extract model parameters (blocked by zero-knowledge property).
- Attacker budget: not stated in paper.
- Phase (training / inference / deployment): inference/deployment (verification of outsourced inference).
- Targeted / untargeted: integrity of a specific computation (targeted correctness claim); parameter confidentiality.
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: not stated in paper.

## Trust assumptions
Verifier is honest but classical and cannot run quantum computations. The cloud quantum executor is untrusted for correctness. Security rests on the underlying ZK proof system (Halo2/Pasta curves) soundness and zero-knowledge — assumed, not proven here. Results are validated only under noise-free simulation (no NISQ noise modeled).

## Attack or failure mechanism
Failure prevented: (a) a remote quantum service silently returning an incorrect inference (result forgery / verifier-gaming by an untrusted host); (b) leakage of secret model parameters to the verifier. The technical challenge/risk is that mapping PQCs to prime-field arithmetic introduces discretization/approximation error, which could break the correspondence between the proven circuit and genuine quantum computation.

## Proposed defense or method
Arithmetic encoding of PQCs into prime-field circuits: quantum amplitude vectors decomposed into real/imaginary parts, mapped to fixed-point over a prime field; parameterized gates U3(θ,ϕ,λ) and CU3 modeled as linear transforms with low-degree polynomial approximations of trig functions and masking-enforced control logic. zkQML enforces agreement between original and arithmetic-encoded PQC outputs within tolerance ε (measured by fidelity and total-variation distance), then generates a non-interactive classical ZK proof that hides parameters — a cryptographic verifiability + parameter-confidentiality defense.

## Datasets and benchmarks
A four-qubit PQC-based QML inference model trained on MNIST. PQC uses the U3CU3 design (single-qubit rotations + entangling gates), depth varied 1→4. Simulation via TorchQuantum v0.2.0; ZK via Halo2 v0.3 (Rust v1.89.0, Pasta curves). Hardware: Intel i7-11700, 32 GB RAM.

## Evaluation methodology
Measure how closely the prime-field (arithmetic-encoded) PQC approximates the actual quantum PQC as circuit depth scales 1–4; visualize state evolution on the Bloch sphere vs quantum-simulator markers; quantify fidelity and TV distance at each depth. Evaluated under noise-free simulation only.

## Metrics
Fidelity (global state similarity, →1 best) and total-variation distance (→0 best). Reported: fidelity above 0.9996 and TV distance below 0.01 for depths 1–4 (e.g., depth-1 fidelity 0.9997500, TV 0.0089895; depth-3 fidelity 0.9996525, TV 0.0062066). Proof-generation time / proof size / verification cost are not reported in the text.

## Main findings
Author claims: (1) first framework for non-interactive, classical-verifier proofs of QML inference; (2) prime-field arithmetic encoding of PQCs preserves inference consistency (fidelity >0.9996, TV <0.01) with negligible error as depth grows to 4; (3) a prototype shows the practicality of privacy-preserving quantum inference where parameters stay hidden and verification is entirely classical.

## Negative results
None reported as failures. Author explicitly bounds the claim to noise-free simulation and notes real NISQ hardware will require noise modeling and proof calibration (open problem, not yet demonstrated).

## Limitations stated by the authors
Validation is under noise-free simulation only; extending to real NISQ hardware needs robust noise modeling and proof calibration. Arithmetic encoding "inevitably introduces discretization and approximation error" (bounded by ε here). Scale beyond 4 qubits / MNIST is not shown.

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis)_
- Tiny setting (4 qubits, depth ≤4, MNIST); no evidence the fidelity/TV bounds hold at practically useful circuit sizes.
- No proving-time, proof-size, or verifier-time numbers — "succinctness/practicality" is asserted but not quantified for the ZK layer.
- Security (soundness/zero-knowledge) is inherited from Halo2 and not independently analyzed; parameter-hiding is claimed via the ZK property, not empirically probed against an extraction attempt.
- The classical arithmetic circuit only *approximates* quantum computation; a proof of the approximation is not a proof of the genuine quantum execution on real hardware — a residual trust gap the authors acknowledge only for NISQ noise.
- Not an agentic system; relevance is by analogy to verifiable/confidential outsourced inference.

## Design implications
Demonstrates a pattern for making outsourced/opaque inference *verifiable to a weaker (classical) verifier* while hiding model internals — directly analogous to wanting cryptographic attestation that a remote model server executed the claimed computation without revealing weights. Encode-to-arithmetic-circuit + ZK is the design lever.

## Implementation implications
Non-arithmetic operations (trig, control logic, quantum amplitudes) must be re-expressed over a prime field with low-degree polynomial approximations and masking; an ε-tolerance agreement check between original and encoded computation is needed to preserve correctness before proving.

## Evaluation implications
Faithfulness of the arithmetic encoding must be measured (fidelity/TV) as a precondition for trusting the proof; ZK-layer cost (prove/verify/size) should also be reported — a gap here to close in follow-on work.

## Deployment implications
Applicable to untrusted cloud inference where clients lack the specialized (here quantum) hardware but need integrity + parameter confidentiality. Not deployment-ready: shown only in noise-free simulation at toy scale; real-hardware noise and proof cost remain unaddressed.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Provides result-integrity verification (detect incorrect outsourced inference) rather than runtime monitoring; a failed proof is the incident signal. No abuse/rate-limiting mechanisms discussed.

## Applicability boundaries
_(where to and NOT to generalize)_
**Adjacent** to agent security: a verifiable-inference + model-confidentiality technique (ZK proofs of outsourced computation) transferable to trust in remote model servers, but the paper is about quantum ML, not LLMs, agents, tools, or MCP. Do NOT read the >0.9996 fidelity as a real-hardware or large-scale guarantee — it is noise-free simulation at 4 qubits, and ZK cost is unmeasured.
