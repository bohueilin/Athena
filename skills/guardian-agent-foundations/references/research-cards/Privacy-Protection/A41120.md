## Research question
Does machine unlearning (selective forgetting) actually deliver the privacy protection it promises, or does it introduce new privacy attack surfaces — and can existing unlearning-induced privacy attacks/defenses be compared fairly under one standardized benchmark?

## Problem definition
Unlearning-induced privacy attacks are each proposed under disparate experimental settings, so each appears to beat its predecessors under its own tailored conditions, yielding overly optimistic, inconsistent assessments. There is no standardized, reproducible benchmark of the empirical privacy risks that unlearning creates. Unlearning produces two model versions (original θ and unlearned θ_u), whose discrepancy can leak information; the unlearned model may retain residual risk; and deployment operations (fine-tuning, quantization) can reactivate memorized knowledge. Risks affect both the unlearning data D_u and the retain data D_r.

## System or model being studied
PrivUB (Privacy Vulnerabilities in Machine Unlearning Benchmark): a structured taxonomy + evaluation harness covering three phases (training T, unlearning U, deployment via fine-tuning F or quantization Q). Covers deep-learning models and LLMs.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: infer membership (was a sample trained-then-unlearned, or in the retain set), reconstruct unlearned data, or extract retained knowledge after unlearning.
- Adversary knowledge/access (varies per attack, taxonomized in Table 1): from label-only / posterior / top-k posterior access to model-weights (white-box); some assume an auxiliary dataset D_aux and shadow models; some access both pre-trained and unlearned models.
- Adversary capabilities: two attacking-tool families — perturbing the unlearned model (e.g., fine-tuning θ_u to θ̃_u) and perturbing the unlearned data (e.g., adversarial suffixes on D_u) — usable individually or combined.
- Attacker budget: sensitivity to number of attack samples is explicitly studied.
- Phase: spans unlearning and deployment (fine-tuning/quantization), targeting inference-time privacy leakage.
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted at specific victim data (D_u or D_r).
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: includes adaptive elements (attackers craft malicious unlearning subsets, perturb data/model); defenses include adversarially-trained/robust unlearning.

## Trust assumptions
Standard unlearning goal: θ_u should be indistinguishable from a model retrained on retain data D_r (exact unlearning ε=δ=0; approximate unlearning bounded ε,δ). The benchmark treats these guarantees as the property under test rather than assumed. Attacker may hold auxiliary data and/or white-box weights depending on the scenario.

## Attack or failure mechanism
Three vulnerability types benchmarked: (M) Membership inference — for D_u (was it trained then removed) and for D_r (removing a crafted "least-private" subset increases D_r membership exposure); (R) Data reconstruction — white-box gradient-matching of the θ vs θ_u difference to recover unlearned samples (most severe leakage); (K) Knowledge leakage — measuring retained predictive accuracy on D_u/domain data after unlearning, amplified by perturbing model or data. Deployment (fine-tuning/quantization) can reactivate memorized knowledge.

## Proposed defense or method
The benchmark itself (standardized, reproducible evaluation) is the contribution, plus a structured taxonomy across dimensions: privacy-vulnerability type, victim-data type, victim-model type, attacking tool, threat model, model architecture. It also benchmarks existing defenses (Table 2): mutual-information minimization between representation and unlearning data (Wang et al. 2025b, vs MIA and reconstruction on D_u); adversarial suffix + latent adversarial unlearning to suppress knowledge leakage (Yuan et al. 2025); sharpness-aware minimization robust unlearning (Fan et al. 2025); tampering-attack resistance TAR (Tamirisa et al. 2025).

## Datasets and benchmarks

Datasets referenced include CIFAR-10/100, CelebA, medical image data, text classification/summarization sets (e.g., XSum), and LLM unlearning suites (e.g., TOFU, WMDP referenced). Exact per-experiment splits are in the truncated portion.

## Evaluation methodology
Instantiate representative attacks per taxonomy cell (grounded with a specific threat model each), run across victim data (D_u, D_r), victim models (deep learning + LLMs), unlearning methods, and attacking tools; evaluate defenses in the same structured manner. Cross-model-type generalization tested (DL↔LLM attack transfer). Sensitivity to number of attack samples analyzed.

## Metrics
10 task-specific metrics (membership-inference success/AUC-style, reconstruction quality, knowledge-leakage/retained-accuracy proxies); exact metric names/values in the truncated experimental section (not fully in extracted text). Findings are stated qualitatively.

## Main findings
(Author claims) (1) Combining attacking tools (perturb unlearned model + perturb unlearned data) improves attack effectiveness. (2) Data-perturbation tools designed for knowledge leakage can further boost membership-inference attacks. (3) Fine-tuning induces more severe privacy risk than quantization during deployment. (4) With proper adaptation, existing attacks generalize across model types — attacks built for deep-learning models transfer to LLMs and vice versa, retaining strong performance. (5) Existing defenses generally lack robustness; some are highly sensitive to the number of attack samples, giving inconsistent protection.

## Negative results
(Author-reported) Defenses are not robust across settings; protection can be inconsistent depending on attack-sample count — i.e., current defenses do not reliably close the unlearning-induced privacy gap.

## Limitations stated by the authors
The work is positioned as the first benchmark and a foundation; it standardizes evaluation rather than proposing a new defense. It highlights that unlearning may not provide anticipated privacy protection and can introduce new attack surfaces (framed as a call for better methods).

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis — not author claims)_
- Benchmark breadth (11 datasets × 10 models × 10 unlearning methods × 21 attacks/defenses) risks shallow per-cell tuning; whether each attack is run at its strongest is hard to verify from the extracted text.
- Many severe results assume white-box weight access (reconstruction, weight-perturbation attacks), which bounds real-world applicability to open-weight deployments.
- Metric definitions and numeric results are in the truncated section, limiting independent assessment of effect sizes.
- Cross-type transfer ("attacks generalize DL↔LLM") is a strong claim whose adaptation details are not in the extracted text.
- No standardized privacy budget (ε,δ) is enforced across compared unlearning methods, so "risk" comparisons partly reflect heterogeneous unlearning strengths.

## Design implications
Do not treat unlearning as a privacy guarantee. Because θ vs θ_u discrepancy is itself a leak, systems that support "forget my data" (including agent memory deletion) must consider that publishing/serving both versions, or later fine-tuning/quantizing, can re-expose the "forgotten" data. Least-privilege on model-weight and dual-version access is a control point.

## Implementation implications
Deployment operations after unlearning matter: prefer quantization over fine-tuning if minimizing reactivation risk (fine-tuning worsened leakage). Combine defenses, since single defenses are sample-count-sensitive; monitor attacker-sample thresholds.

## Evaluation implications
Provides a standardized, apples-to-apples harness so new unlearning attacks/defenses can be compared under fixed victim data/model/tool settings — countering the "each beats predecessors under its own setting" bias. Evaluators should test both D_u and D_r victims and both perturb-model and perturb-data tools, plus deployment-phase reactivation.

## Deployment implications
Right-to-be-forgotten compliance based on approximate unlearning may leave residual, exploitable privacy risk; regulated deployments should pair unlearning with robust defenses and treat the unlearned model's weights/outputs as sensitive.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Track membership-inference / reconstruction / knowledge-leakage signals after each unlearning request and after any fine-tune/quantize step; watch defense sensitivity to attack-sample count. Retain evidence of what was unlearned and when for audit.

## Applicability boundaries
Applies to machine-unlearning privacy risk in DL and LLM models across MIA, reconstruction, and knowledge-leakage attacks. Adjacent to agent security: directly relevant to agent-memory forgetting, model updates, and "delete my data" flows, but not about prompt/tool/agent-runtime attacks specifically. Reconstruction/weight-perturbation results assume white-box access and should not be generalized to black-box API-only deployments without re-evaluation.
