## Research question
Can copyright infringement in a deployed text-to-image diffusion model — i.e., whether the model has memorized/reproduces a specific copyrighted concept — be detected post-hoc, robustly, and with a theoretical grounding, without access to the original training data or the triggering prompt, by reframing infringement through the lens of differential privacy?

## Problem definition
Diffusion models (e.g., Stable Diffusion) can memorize and reproduce copyrighted training content. Existing detectors are (a) model-level (CopyScope: FID-Shapley attribution) that cannot localize to specific concepts/samples, or (b) prompt-based, which are fragile to prompt phrasing, model updates, and sampling randomness, and lack interpretability/reproducibility. Need: a per-concept, theory-grounded, training-data-free, prompt-free detector.

## System or model being studied
D-Plus-Minus (DPM), a post-hoc detection framework for text-to-image diffusion models. It reframes infringement as violation of "conditional publicity" (a DP-analog: how much a model's output depends on including vs excluding a specific training concept) and defines a "conditional sensitivity" metric (analog of DP local sensitivity). DPM fine-tunes the target model in two opposing branches — a learning branch (G_{D+}, memorize the concept) and an unlearning branch (G_{D−}, forget it) — over a neighborhood of images U(x_i) semantically extracted from the target image, then compares CLIP-image-embedding outputs to the original model. To isolate concept-specific shift from global fine-tuning drift, it computes confidence over orthogonal prompt distributions (orthogonal sensitivity) and merges branches into a confidence score f(x_i) ∈ [0,1].

## Threat model
- Adversary objective (framed as detection target): an infringing model that has memorized copyrighted content and reproduces it; the "adversary" is the (possibly unknown) trainer who included copyrighted samples. DPM is the forensic detector.
- Adversary knowledge: DPM operates under white-box access to a pretrained model, no input prompt, no training data.
- Adversary access: DPM (the detector) needs white-box model weights to run learning/unlearning fine-tuning branches.
- Adversary capabilities: the underlying privacy threats motivating the work include membership inference (>60% success cited from prior work) and training-data extraction/reproduction of individual training images.
- Attacker budget: not stated (detector-side cost = two fine-tuning branches over neighborhood images across timesteps).
- Phase: memorization occurs at training; detection is post-hoc at deployment.
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted (per-concept / per-image infringement query).
- Digital / physical: digital (generative model IP).
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: non-adaptive — no adaptive evader (e.g., a model trained to hide memorization from DPM) is evaluated in the provided text.

## Trust assumptions
Trusts white-box access to the deployed model; trusts CLIP image embeddings as a faithful "query function" M(·) for measuring output shift; assumes a neighborhood U(c_i) of internet-collected images approximates the copyrighted concept; assumes fine-tuning inclusion/exclusion simulates the presence/absence of the training data point. Explicitly does NOT assume access to training data or the original prompt.

## Attack or failure mechanism
The underlying leakage: diffusion models exhibit "almost no conditional differential privacy" w.r.t. training data (high "publicity"), so a single copyrighted concept can drastically alter outputs (ϵ>200 characterized as a substantial privacy violation). Infringement = memorization made observable as a large conditional-sensitivity shift; non-infringement = output distribution invariant to inclusion/exclusion of the sample.

## Proposed defense or method
DPM detection (see System). Formalizes conditional publicity (Def. 2), copyright infringement / non-infringement (Def. 3–4), and detection as confidence scoring (Def. 5) with conditional sensitivity CS(M, x̂_i) (Eq. 10). Procedure: (1) extract & filter a core copyright-protectable concept from the target image; (2) collect neighborhood + orthogonal images and build image-prompt pairs with a unique identifier ("a photo of [V] person"); (3) run learning & unlearning branches; (4) measure conditional sensitivity; (5) statistically align empirical to ideal sensitivity via orthogonal reference; (6) merge branches → confidence score.

## Datasets and benchmarks
CIDD (Copyright Infringement Detection Dataset, introduced): covers three high-risk categories — human face, architecture, arts painting — with potentially-infringing and non-infringing content, controllable samples, and aligned clean pairs, for LVMs/LVLMs.

## Evaluation methodology
Evaluated on four text-to-image models: Stable Diffusion v1.4, SDXL, SANA, FLUX. Reports quantitative (ROC-AUC, SoftAcc) and qualitative (branch-divergence visualizations, conditional-sensitivity plots) results, plus ablation/robustness studies (branch merging, multi-timestep, image degradation). Extension to LLMs/LVLMs left as future work.

## Metrics
ROC-AUC (infringed vs non-infringed discrimination) and SoftAcc (stricter numeric alignment of confidence to ground-truth label, penalizing deviation). Reported: weighted-average AUC > 80% across all four models; SoftAcc > 72%. Merging two branches and multiple timesteps improves/stabilizes detection; image degradation has little effect on performance.

## Main findings
(1) First to cast copyright infringement (and its detection) in differential-privacy terms, defining conditional sensitivity. (2) DPM detects infringement without training data or prompts, using paired learning/unlearning fine-tuning branches + orthogonal-sensitivity normalization. (3) Across four models, weighted-avg AUC > 80% and SoftAcc > 72%, with robustness to image degradation and gains from branch/timestep merging. (4) CIDD provides a standardized benchmark.

## Negative results
Detection AUC (~80%+) is strong but not near-perfect, and varies across classes (attributed to CLIP generalization, diffusion-model configs, and fine-tuning timesteps) — an honest acknowledgment that performance is class/model-dependent. LLM/LVLM detection is not yet demonstrated (deferred).

## Limitations stated by the authors
Requires white-box access to the model. Detection performance varies by class due to CLIP capability and model/timestep configuration. Extension to LLMs/LVLMs is future work. (Ablation/robustness details are in the appendix/extended version.)

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis)_
- White-box requirement limits real-world forensic use where alleged infringers expose only APIs (black-box); the practical scenario for litigation is often black-box.
- No adaptive adversary: a model owner aware of DPM could fine-tune to suppress conditional-sensitivity signals (memorization-hiding) — untested.
- The DP framing is analogical, not a formal guarantee about the deployed model; "conditional sensitivity" is an empirical CLIP-embedding statistic, so ϵ>200 is illustrative, not a certified privacy bound.
- Ground-truth "infringement" labels in CIDD are constructed; legal infringement is a normative judgment DPM approximates via similarity/memorization, not adjudicates.
- Running two fine-tuning branches per queried concept across timesteps is computationally heavy at scale.

## Design implications
Memorization/provenance auditing can be operationalized as a sensitivity-to-inclusion measurement rather than prompt-triggering — a robustness/interpretability win. The learning-vs-unlearning "differential" is a reusable probe: measure how a targeted include/exclude fine-tune moves outputs to infer training-data dependence. Directly relevant to training-data-provenance and IP-governance layers of an AI stack.

## Implementation implications
For provenance/audit tooling: DPM shows a training-data-free, prompt-free path to per-concept attribution, but requires white-box weights and heavy compute — implementers should budget for per-query fine-tuning and treat scores as evidence, not verdicts. Orthogonal-prompt normalization is a practical trick to separate concept-specific from global fine-tune drift.

## Evaluation implications
Copyright/memorization detectors should be benchmarked across multiple model families (SD1.4/SDXL/SANA/FLUX here) and stress-tested for robustness (degradation) — and, as a gap, against adaptive memorization-hiding adversaries and black-box access. CIDD is a reusable benchmark for this.

## Deployment implications
Supports post-hoc IP-compliance/forensic workflows (NIST AI RMF, EU AI Act, Chinese GenAI measures are cited as motivating regulation). Residual risk: white-box-only + non-adaptive evaluation means a determined infringer with API-only exposure or memorization-hiding training may evade detection.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Provides interpretable, per-concept evidence (conditional-sensitivity divergence, learning/unlearning visualizations) suitable as audit records for infringement incidents — an evidence-logging pattern for generative-model IP governance.

## Applicability boundaries
_(where these findings should and should NOT be generalized)_
Adjacent-to-core for AI/ML security and privacy: it is a genuine memorization/privacy-leakage detection method (membership-inference / training-data-reconstruction flavored) grounded in DP, directly relevant to training-data provenance, IP protection, and audit evidence. Boundaries: results are for text-to-image diffusion under WHITE-BOX access and NON-adaptive conditions; do NOT generalize the AUC to black-box APIs, to LLM/LVLM text infringement (explicitly future work), or treat the DP framing as a formal guarantee. It is not about prompt-injection or tool/agent runtime security, but its provenance/auditing ideas transfer to agent data-governance.
