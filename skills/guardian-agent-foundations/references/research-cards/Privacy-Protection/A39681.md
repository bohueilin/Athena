## Research question
When machine unlearning is repurposed from privacy-driven data deletion to non-privacy goals (fairness, adversarial robustness), the inherited binary "remove-or-keep" (hard-weighted) framework causes "over-unlearning" and utility loss. Can a soft, per-sample weighting — derived analytically — improve the target metric (fairness/robustness) while preserving utility?

## Problem definition
Traditional unlearning treats each sample as fully removed (ε=−1) or fully kept (0). Repurposed for fairness/robustness this over-corrects: (1) removing the most detrimental (biased/vulnerable) samples does not necessarily improve utility; (2) borderline samples (score ≈ 0) are treated like highly detrimental ones, causing excessive unlearning and even reverse bias; (3) most negative-influence samples (≈70–75%) actually remain in the retained set because algorithms only delete a small subset (~20%). The paper (via counterfactual leave-one-out analysis on CelebA) formalizes over-unlearning and seeks a fine-grained weighting instead of binary removal.

## System or model being studied
A weighted machine-unlearning framework built on influence functions (Koh & Liang 2017). It computes per-sample weighted influence on utility, fairness (Demographic Parity, Equal Opportunity via differentiable surrogates), and robustness (loss on adversarially perturbed samples). Weights ε are found by a convex quadratic program with a closed-form solution; the resulting weights update the model through the influence-function closed form (or first-order multi-step surrogate). Influence is computed on the last layer (convex surrogate) so it generalizes to arbitrary neural nets; applies to first- and second-order unlearning algorithms (e.g., Fisher, Teacher–Student).

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: robustness setting considers a white-box adversary crafting adversarial examples (orthogonal perturbations across a linear decision hyperplane, extendable to FGSM/PGD) to flip predictions; fairness setting has no adversary (bias is a data/model property).
- Adversary knowledge (white/gray/black-box): white-box for the robustness/adversarial-sample crafting.
- Adversary access: perturbs test/validation inputs to measure perturbed accuracy; not attacking the unlearning procedure itself.
- Adversary capabilities: bounded input perturbation of magnitude controlled by γ>1.
- Attacker budget: perturbation magnitude parameter γ.
- Phase (training / inference / deployment): post-training (unlearning is a post-hoc model edit); robustness evaluated at inference.
- Targeted / untargeted: fairness is group-level (sensitive attribute g∈{0,1}); robustness is per-sample perturbation.
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: non-adaptive (fixed perturbation strategy; no adaptive attack against the reweighting).

## Trust assumptions
Assumes access to training data influence estimates and a validation set T for measuring utility/fairness/robustness gradients. Influence-function validity assumes (last-layer) convexity; Theorem 1 requires linear independence of fairness vs. utility gradients and full column rank of the per-sample gradient Jacobian (holds almost surely when n ≫ d and samples are diverse). Trusts that the model owner runs the correction honestly. Reviewer note: this is a model-owner-side quality/governance method, not an adversary-facing privacy defense.

## Attack or failure mechanism
The failure addressed is over-unlearning: binary removal of detrimental samples degrades utility, mishandles borderline samples (flipping them to the unprivileged group and inducing reverse bias), and cannot delete enough negative-influence samples. The mechanism: influence functions estimate each sample's effect on utility/fairness/robustness; hard ε∈{−1,0} is too coarse.

## Proposed defense or method
Soft-weighted unlearning: replace binary ε with continuous per-sample weights ε_j, where I(·)(z_j; ε_j) = −ε_j·I(·)(z_j; −1). Solve a convex QP: minimize Σ I_metric(z_i; ε_i) + λ‖ε‖² subject to Σ I_metric ≥ −Δ (prevent over-correction/reverse bias) and Σ I_util ≤ 0 (preserve utility); closed-form solution given (four conditions). Defense classes: robust_optimization (robustness task), and data-influence-based model correction/rollback (unlearning). Theorem 1 / Corollary 2 prove a Pareto improvement (fairness/robustness ↓ and utility ↓ simultaneously) under stated conditions. Integrable into most existing unlearning algorithms (first- and second-order).

## Datasets and benchmarks
CelebA (face attributes; used for the leave-one-out over-unlearning analysis); references indicate additional datasets (e.g., Bank telemarketing, toxicity detection, CIFAR/other in appendix — not fully enumerated in extracted text). Fairness metrics: Demographic Parity, Equal Opportunity. Robustness via adversarial perturbations (linear-model white-box, extendable to FGSM/PGD).

## Evaluation methodology
Counterfactual leave-one-out (LOO) analysis to characterize over-unlearning (per-sample changes in fairness/robustness vs. utility). Then integrate soft-weighting into existing unlearning algorithms and compare hard- vs soft-weighted schemes on fairness/robustness metrics and utility, plus runtime overhead. Theoretical Pareto-improvement proofs.

## Metrics
Fairness: Demographic Parity, Equal Opportunity (surrogate-differentiable). Robustness: perturbed accuracy (accuracy under adversarial perturbation), distinguished from utility = standard accuracy / test loss. Efficiency: additional wall-clock overhead (reported as "a few seconds").

## Main findings
- Author claim: first work to address over-unlearning specifically when unlearning is used for fairness/robustness (not privacy).
- LOO analysis reveals three concrete over-unlearning pathologies (detrimental-removal ≠ utility gain; borderline samples over-unlearned; most negative-influence samples remain).
- Soft-weighting significantly outperforms hard-weighting on fairness/robustness metrics while alleviating utility decline, with only seconds of extra overhead.
- Theorem 1 / Corollary 2: under linear-independence + convexity + full-rank conditions, weights exist that strictly improve fairness (or robustness) and utility jointly (Pareto improvement).
- Method is algorithm-agnostic (plugs into first- and second-order unlearning methods), enabled by last-layer convex-surrogate influence.

## Negative results
The theoretical guarantee is conditional: Pareto improvement requires linear independence of the metric vs. utility gradients, influence-function validity (convexity), and full-rank Jacobian — conditions the paper argues typically hold on last-layer/convex surrogates but which can fail otherwise. For generative models, the standard last-layer strategy may be ineffective, requiring a diagonal Hessian approximation.

## Limitations stated by the authors
- Influence-function theory is intractable for non-convex models; the method computes influence on the last layer to ensure convexity (justified via last-layer retraining literature), which bounds where guarantees hold.
- For generative models the last-layer approach may not work; a diagonal σI Hessian approximation is proposed as a fallback.
- Fairness has no universal metric; the paper uses group-fairness (DP, EOP) and does not adjudicate among competing fairness definitions.

## Design implications
Data-influence-driven model correction should be graded (soft weights), not binary: fine-grained reweighting can hit a fairness/robustness target while protecting utility, and can be layered onto existing unlearning algorithms rather than replacing them.

## Implementation implications
A convex QP with a closed-form solution makes soft-weighting cheap (seconds); last-layer influence keeps it tractable and model-agnostic; the first-order multi-step surrogate connects it to gradient-ascent-style unlearners. For generative models, use a diagonal-Hessian approximation.

## Evaluation implications
When unlearning is repurposed for non-privacy goals, evaluate utility alongside the target metric (fairness/robustness) — over-unlearning is invisible if only the target metric is reported. LOO counterfactual analysis is a useful diagnostic for hidden utility costs.

## Deployment implications
Post-hoc, low-overhead correction to reduce bias or improve robustness without full retraining, applicable to already-deployed classifiers via last-layer edits. Reviewer caution: using it as a "privacy" unlearning mechanism is unsupported here — it targets fairness/robustness, and certified data removal is out of scope.

## Monitoring and incident implications
The framework's influence scores can flag samples driving bias/vulnerability, supporting audit/monitoring of data quality. It functions as a correction/rollback tool (edit the model toward a fairness/robustness target) rather than a runtime defense.
