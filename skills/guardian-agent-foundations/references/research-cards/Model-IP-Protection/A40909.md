## Research question
Can LLM ownership be verified reliably when the model thief fully controls the suspected model's end-to-end inference,
i.e., when the adversary can inspect/modify weights, collude to unlearn disclosed fingerprint prompt-response pairs,
and manipulate outputs to evade exact-match verification?

## Problem definition
LLM fingerprinting for IP ownership verification under a realistic litigation threat model. Prior passive
fingerprinting lacks forgery resistance (anyone with API access can extract the feature and overclaim); prior proactive
fingerprinting embeds the secret solely in weights (removable under full access) and relies on exact-match verification
(evadable by output manipulation) and on publicly disclosing a prompt-response pair (enabling collusion-based
unlearning). The paper targets verification robustness against these verification-time attacks.

## System or model being studied
iSeal: three components — (1) model registration (authority samples a secret key, initializes a key-generated
encoder, returns selected plaintexts); (2) fingerprint injection (encoder encrypts plaintexts; LLM fine-tuned to
reconstruct Reed-Solomon-Code-encoded targets); (3) ownership verification (judge queries the suspect API and matches
decoded outputs via similarity, with RSC error correction). Key ingredients: an external secret (key-generated encoder
not embedded in the model), cryptographic diffusion/confusion, similarity-based (not exact-match) verification, and an
error-correction module.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: deploy a stolen LLM copy as a paid API and defeat ownership verification (remove/unlearn/evade
  the fingerprint; or overclaim ownership of others' models).
- Adversary knowledge: white-box on the stolen model (full weight read/modify, inference control); knows disclosed
  verification prompt-response pairs; can reverse-engineer exact-match fingerprints.
- Adversary access: full control of the suspected model's end-to-end inference; can collude with other parties to share
  verification prompts.
- Adversary capabilities: collusion-based fingerprint unlearning (fine-tune away disclosed records), output/response
  manipulation to evade matching, overclaim attempts.
- Attacker budget: >10 attacks evaluated; specific budgets in truncated tables.
- Phase: fingerprint injected during training/fine-tuning; attacks and verification at deployment/inference.
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted ownership verification of a specific registered model.
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: adaptive — verification-time attacks (collusion unlearning, response manipulation) are the
  explicit focus.

## Trust assumptions
Judge and registration authority are trusted third parties with black-box API access to suspected and registered
models and no knowledge of internal architecture. The secret key / encoder is held outside the model and not
accessible to the thief. Prompts are exclusively managed and queried by the judge to prevent overclaiming. Assumes the
LLM can be fine-tuned to reconstruct RSC-encoded ciphertext targets and that a similarity threshold separates owned
from non-owned models.

## Attack or failure mechanism
Prior methods fail because: (a) the fingerprint lives only in weights and is removable under full access; (b) exact-
match verification breaks under output manipulation; (c) publicly disclosing a prompt-response pair lets colluders
unlearn or reverse-engineer it, invalidating future claims (e.g., a subsequent lawsuit).

## Proposed defense or method
Encrypted external-secret fingerprinting: key-generated encoder decouples the fingerprint from the model (weight
access alone cannot remove it); cryptographic diffusion/confusion so disclosed prompt-response pairs reveal no usable
information (resisting collusion unlearning/inference); similarity-based verification tolerant to output edits;
Reed-Solomon error correction for recovery when partial manipulation occurs, with theoretical robustness bounds.

## Datasets and benchmarks
12 LLMs used (e.g., LLaMA, Mistral, Vicuna, Pythia, OPT, GPT-J, Amber, mT5 per references); baselines include HuRef,
REEF, ProFLingo, TRAP (passive) and WLM, IF (proactive). Standard NLP/eval suites referenced (SuperGLUE, etc.);
detailed tables truncated in extracted text.

## Evaluation methodology
Compare Fingerprint Success Rate (FSR) of iSeal vs. baselines under >10 attacks including collusion-based unlearning
and response manipulation, in API-only (black-box) verification settings; theoretical analysis of the cryptographic
and error-correction modules.

## Metrics
Fingerprint Success Rate (FSR) / verification success rate; robustness under enumerated attacks; (comparison table
axes: suspected-model access, forgery resistance, external secret, verification robustness).

## Main findings
- iSeal reports 100% FSR on 12 LLMs against more than 10 attacks under the evaluated conditions, while baselines drop
  to 0% under unlearning and response manipulation (author claim).
- The external secret (key-generated encoder) prevents fingerprint removal via weight access alone.
- Similarity-based verification plus RSC error correction preserves verification under output manipulation, with a
  stated theoretical robustness bound.
- Cryptographic diffusion/confusion is argued to make collusion-based unlearning ineffective because removing a few
  disclosed records cannot erase the full prompt-response relationship.

## Negative results
None reported for iSeal; baseline failures under unlearning/manipulation are the contrast. Utility/quality impact of

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis)_
- Security rests on trusted judge and registration authority and on secrecy of the key/encoder; compromise of the
  authority or key escrow is out of the evaluated model.
- "100% FSR" is under the paper's evaluated attack set; a stronger adaptive adversary aware of the RSC/similarity
  design (e.g., targeted distillation, heavy fine-tuning, or output-distribution shaping) is not exhaustively bounded
  in the extracted text.
- Fine-tuning-based fingerprint injection may affect model utility; the utility-vs-robustness trade-off across 12
  models is only partly visible in the extracted text.
- Assumes the thief cannot detect/strip the injected reconstruction behavior; detectability of the fingerprinted
  outputs is not fully characterized here.

## Design implications
Directly relevant to agent/LLM IP protection: bind ownership evidence to an *external secret* (key/encoder) rather than
weights alone, use similarity + error-correction instead of brittle exact match, and keep verification prompts under
a trusted verifier to prevent overclaim — a template for tamper-evident model-identity/attestation.

## Implementation implications
Ownership verification should assume the adversary controls inference end-to-end; verification logic (thresholds,
error-correction, key custody) belongs to a trusted third party, not the deployed model.

## Evaluation implications
Fingerprinting evaluations must include adaptive verification-time attacks (collusion unlearning, response
manipulation, overclaim) and black-box API-only access, not just clean exact-match success.

## Deployment implications
A passed verification is legal/ownership *evidence* whose strength depends on trusted-authority integrity and key
secrecy; residual risk from authority compromise, distillation, and aggressive fine-tuning should be governed and
disclosed.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Fits IP-theft incident response (litigation/audit): reliable black-box ownership checks against deployed suspect APIs;
audit trail should record key custody, judge queries, and RSC-decoded matches.

## Applicability boundaries
Scope: black-box LLM ownership verification / anti-theft under an adversary controlling inference. Core to agent/LLM
security (model identity, IP protection, adversarial verification). Do not extend the numbers to modalities beyond text
LLMs or to threat models where the judge/authority or key is compromised.
