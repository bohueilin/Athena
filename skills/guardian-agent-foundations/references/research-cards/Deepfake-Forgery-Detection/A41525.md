## Research question
How can a classroom game centered on adversarially spoofing an image classifier (rather than building models) cultivate critical, transformative AI literacy — teaching learners aged 10–15 about model brittleness, bias, robustness, and accountability?

## Problem definition
K–12 AI literacy that goes beyond model-building to critical interrogation: students induce high-confidence misclassifications by manipulating their real-world appearance/environment, and use XAI heatmaps and training-data inspection to understand why classifiers fail. This is a pedagogical design/resource paper, not a detection method.

## System or model being studied
"Breakable Machine": two browser apps (teacher projector app + student mobile app) over WebRTC peer-to-peer. Classifier is MobileNet V2, fine-tuned separately per challenge/label. XAI view uses Class Activation Maps (CAMs) to show salient regions; a shared leaderboard displays each student's highest-confidence result; a training-data browser gives spoofing hints.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective (pedagogical role-play): the student acts as attacker, seeking to trigger a high-confidence misclassification for a target label (e.g., "doctor", "astronaut").
- Adversary knowledge: gray/white-box in effect — learners can inspect the training data and CAM saliency to guide spoofing.
- Adversary access: physical-world input to the camera (appearance, props, background, lighting); no access to model weights/gradients.
- Adversary capabilities: real-world (embodied) manipulation, not pixel-space perturbation; contrasted explicitly with synthetic pixel-space attacks (Papernot et al. 2017).
- Attacker budget: everyday props and classroom devices; no compute budget for gradient attacks.
- Phase: inference/deployment (attacking a deployed classifier).
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted (specific target label).
- Digital / physical: physical-world spoofing via camera.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: adaptive by the human learner (iterative trial-and-error guided by CAM/leaderboard).

## Trust assumptions
Assumes classifiers are fallible, human-made sociotechnical systems that can be probed and tricked; the pedagogy deliberately treats misclassification as a learning opportunity, not a bug. Privacy assumption: all data stay local (GDPR-compliant), profile pictures replaced with AI-generated images, session data auto-deleted.

## Attack or failure mechanism
Image classifiers are highly sensitive to non-semantic visual features (eyeglasses, headwear, skin tone, background objects, lighting). Learners exploit this by adding props/altering environment to push confidence high on a wrong label — demonstrating that confident predictions are not necessarily correct and that models attend to spurious cues (e.g., an empty glass classified as "astronaut" due to the visor-like round glass in training images).

## Proposed defense or method
No defense proposed; the contribution is a pedagogical resource and design rationale. The "method" is a structured classroom activity (four pedagogical activities: analyze classification, explore fragility, experiment with adversarial tactics, reflect on societal consequences) situated in the CEDE model and mapped to AI-literacy frameworks (AI4K12, Long & Magerko, UNESCO, SEAME).

## Datasets and benchmarks
Per-challenge fine-tuned MobileNet V2 classifiers with small labeled image/label datasets browsable by students; no standard forgery-detection benchmark used. No quantitative dataset statistics reported.

## Evaluation methodology
None yet — the paper explicitly states empirical evaluation of learning outcomes is scheduled for future work. It presents design, pedagogical foundations, screenshots, and classroom use scenarios only.

## Metrics
None (no learning-outcome measurement reported). The only in-app metric is the classifier's confidence score, used pedagogically to show confidence != correctness.

## Main findings
- Author claim (design rationale, not measured): framing spoofing as critical inquiry can teach trust, robustness, brittleness, feature saliency, and accountability, repositioning learners from passive users to active interrogators.
- Real-world embodied spoofing is a concrete entry point to AI safety/robustness concepts, distinct from pixel-space adversarial attacks.

## Negative results
Not applicable — no empirical study conducted.

## Limitations stated by the authors
Empirical evaluation of learning outcomes is future work; the paper offers only learning objectives and theoretical rationale. Requires prior familiarity with image-classification concepts.

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis — clearly not author claims)_
- No evidence yet that the game improves AI-literacy outcomes; all pedagogical claims are unvalidated.
- Not a security/forgery-detection technique — it neither detects nor defends against deepfakes; it teaches evasion of a classifier.
- Generalization of learning depends heavily on teacher facilitation and the specific fine-tuned classifiers/datasets used.
- CAM saliency can itself mislead learners about model reasoning (explanation-faithfulness caveat not discussed).

## Design implications
Adversarial "red-teaming as play" is a viable way to build intuition about model brittleness and spurious-feature reliance; XAI saliency + training-data inspection make the attack surface legible to non-experts.

## Implementation implications
Local, peer-to-peer, GDPR-conscious design (no external data egress, ephemeral session data, AI-generated placeholder avatars) is a practical pattern for deploying AI tools with minors.

## Evaluation implications
Any adoption should pair the tool with a real learning-outcome study; confidence-score-as-metric is a teaching device, not a rigor measure.

## Deployment implications
Intended for grade 4–9 classrooms as part of a larger project (e.g., CEDE); low infrastructure needs (browser + local network). Not a deployable security control.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Minimal — a self-contained classroom app; the salient security lesson is that deployed classifiers can be confidently wrong under real-world spoofing, relevant when such classifiers are used for consequential decisions (the paper cites automated hiring/interview scoring on spurious cues).
