## Research question
Can an open-source LLM be released so that it retains full utility for legitimate use but becomes non-mergeable — i.e., it cannot be combined via model-merging (Task Arithmetic, TIES-MERGING, DARE) with other homologous fine-tunes to steal its specialized capabilities — without relying on external hardware/keys and without accuracy loss?

## Problem definition
"Model merging stealing": free-riders merge a restrictively-licensed open-access model (e.g. CC BY-NC-ND) into their own homologous models to appropriate its capabilities cheaply and untraceably (merging can also erase watermarks). Existing defenses fail to satisfy three properties simultaneously: (C1) Proactivity — prevent merging even with full white-box access; (C2) Compatibility — no external components (keys/TEE) required; (C3) Security with Utility — protect weights without degrading performance.

## System or model being studied
MergeBarrier, a plug-and-play weight transformation applied to a fine-tuned model before release. It targets Linear Mode Connectivity (LMC): homologous models fine-tuned from the same base share a low-loss basin, enabling merge interpolation. MergeBarrier displaces the protected model out of that basin while preserving its function: (a) Attention — a shared orthogonal projection P applied to query and key weights (Wq P, Wk P), optimized to maximize deviation of the merged weights from the original basin; because P Pᵀ = I the projection cancels in the paired QKᵀ computation, so the model's own output is unchanged. (b) FFN — activation-function-expansion (Taylor) reparameterization rewriting FFN computation into functionally-equivalent polynomial parameters that hide the original weights; the remainder error is argued to reduce weight inversion to a Learning-With-Errors (LWE), NP-hard problem. RSVD accelerates the eigendecomposition.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: acquire the defender's specialized capabilities cheaply by merging the protected open-source model with adversary-controlled homologous models (model theft / unauthorized adaptation for commercial gain).
- Adversary knowledge: white-box — attacker has the defender's open-source model, the pretrained base, and even the defense-strategy papers.
- Adversary access: full download of released weights; ability to fine-tune own homologous models and run any merging algorithm; can attempt weight alignment/permutation reversal and scaling inversion.
- Adversary capabilities: limited data and compute (cannot train a strong target from scratch), so relies on merging; can optimize the merged model's performance.
- Attacker budget: low compute (merging is cheap); bounded — cannot solve the LWE/NP-hard inversion.
- Phase: post-training / model distribution / deployment (protection applied pre-release; attack at redistribution time).
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted appropriation of a specific protected model's skills.
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: adaptive white-box adversary is explicitly assumed (knows the defense).

## Trust assumptions
Defender controls and modifies their own model before release and trusts the transformation is applied faithfully. Assumes attackers begin from the same widely-used pretrained base (homology) — the mechanism depends on this. Assumes no trusted hardware/key infrastructure is available (open-source setting).

## Attack or failure mechanism
Model merging works only when expert models lie in a shared low-loss basin (LMC). By transforming attention/FFN weights so the protected model leaves that basin (while its own forward pass is preserved by orthogonal cancellation / functional-equivalence), any interpolation with homologous models lands in a high-loss region, so the merged model's task performance collapses. Passive defenses (watermark/fingerprint) only detect post-hoc and are erased by merging; key/TEE defenses need infrastructure incompatible with open-source; prior emulator/reparam defenses (Offsite-Tuning, TaylorMLP, PaRaMS) trade off utility or leave most weights exposed.

## Proposed defense or method
MergeBarrier (above): attention orthogonal projection + FFN activation-expansion reparameterization, with an orthogonality optimization (relaxed top-k eigen-direction flip) and RSVD acceleration. Claimed to be the first formal definition of and proactive defense against model-merging stealing, preserving performance while degrading merged-model performance; the FFN remainder error is argued to make weight inversion NP-hard (LWE), and the transformations are further claimed to "ensure differential privacy."

## Datasets and benchmarks
Protects expert models fine-tuned across different domains and evaluates merged-model performance on the respective tasks (e.g. code/math/instruction-following expert domains referenced via Code Llama, WizardMath, WizardLM, AlpacaEval-style evaluation in the reference set). Exact benchmark table is in the truncated/appendix portion; visible text describes protecting domain experts and measuring accuracy of merged vs. original.

## Evaluation methodology
Compare (1) original protected model accuracy (should be preserved) vs. (2) accuracy of models an attacker obtains by merging the protected model with homologous experts (should drop). Compared against passive (watermark/fingerprint), authorization-based (key/TEE), and emulator-based (Offsite-Tuning, TaylorMLP, PaRaMS) baselines on the three properties (Table 1). Theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–2) for orthogonal-matrix existence/optimality and the LWE hardness argument.

## Metrics
Task accuracy of protected model (utility preservation) and of merged model (protection strength / accuracy drop); qualitative property matrix (Proactivity / Compatibility / Security-with-Utility).

## Main findings
(Author claims, under evaluated conditions.) MergeBarrier prevents model-merging stealing with negligible accuracy loss to the legitimate model, causing significant performance drops when attackers merge the protected model; it satisfies all three properties where prior methods each miss at least one; theoretically the FFN reparameterization noise reduces weight inversion to an NP-hard LWE problem.

## Negative results
None highlighted as failures; the paper positions MergeBarrier as strictly dominating baselines on the three-property matrix. (Reviewer: absence of reported failure cases is itself a caution.)

## Limitations stated by the authors
The mechanism depends on homology (same pretrained base); its scope is model-merging stealing specifically. (The visible text does not enumerate an extensive limitations section.)

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis)_ (1) The "differential privacy" claim in the conclusion is asserted without a stated ε/δ budget or DP mechanism in the visible text — treat as unsubstantiated / imprecise. (2) "Strong security guarantee" rests on an LWE reduction of one inversion path; it does not preclude other extraction routes (e.g., distillation/query-based extraction, or partial-capability transfer). (3) Protection is defeated conceptually if the attacker does not need exact weights but only behavioral cloning. (4) Evaluated on specific expert domains/architectures; generality across many merging algorithms and larger models beyond the reported set is not fully shown in the extracted text. (5) No defense against a defender-side supply-chain compromise (transformation stripped before release).

## Design implications
Model IP/anti-theft is an active-defense problem, not just watermark-after-the-fact: for open-weight release, embedding merge-resistance (basin displacement) into the weights is a candidate control where TEE/keys are infeasible. Relevant to the model-distribution supply chain that agents depend on.

## Implementation implications
Apply transformations across the whole transformer block (both attention and FFN) — partial protection (single layer) leaves exposure (their critique of TaylorMLP). Use RSVD to keep the transformation tractable at LLM scale. Pair with (not replace) provenance/watermarking for traceability, since MergeBarrier prevents but does not attribute.

## Evaluation implications
Anti-merging defenses must be evaluated against adaptive white-box attackers who know the defense and attempt alignment/permutation/scaling inversion; utility preservation must be measured on the legitimate task, and protection measured as merged-model degradation across multiple merging algorithms.

## Deployment implications
Enables restrictive-license (CC BY-NC-ND) open-weight releases with technical, not merely legal, enforcement of non-merging. Deployers relying on merged community models should note that protected models will silently fail after merging.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Because merging can erase watermarks, ownership monitoring cannot rely on post-hoc marks alone; proactive non-mergeability plus provenance logging of released artifacts is advisable. Flag anomalous accuracy collapse in merged models as a possible protection trigger rather than a bug.

## Applicability boundaries
Applies to protecting open-weight LLM IP against capability theft via model merging among homologous fine-tunes. It does NOT protect against behavioral distillation, query-based extraction, or non-homologous appropriation, and the DP/"guarantee" language should not be read as a formal privacy or unbreakability proof. Adjacent to agent security via the model-distribution/supply-chain surface.

## Confidence notes
