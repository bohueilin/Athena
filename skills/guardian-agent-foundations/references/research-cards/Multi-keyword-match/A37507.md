## Research question
Can data owners protect their images from unauthorized diffusion-model personalization by *actively redirecting* what a
fine-tuned model learns toward a user-specified target concept (Targeted Data Protection, TDP), rather than merely
degrading output quality (Untargeted Data Protection, UDP)?

## Problem definition
Text-to-image diffusion models can be fine-tuned (e.g., DreamBooth) on scraped personal images to reproduce a person's
identity, enabling deepfakes, non-consensual content, and copyright infringement. Existing protection perturbations
(UDP) only passively degrade quality, leaving residual identity traces, providing no verifiable proof of protection, and
no traceability. Prior TDP attempts (T-ASPL) use snapshot-based optimization whose protective influence is "diluted"
as fine-tuning proceeds, so genuine targeted identity redirection has not been reliably achieved.

## System or model being studied
Protector-side method (TAFAP) that crafts imperceptible adversarial perturbations for images before release. Attacker
model studied: Stable Diffusion (SD1.4) personalized via DreamBooth + LoRA (also cross-tested on SD2.1 and without
LoRA). Protection mechanism: repurposes Matching Training Trajectory (MTT, from dataset distillation) so the fine-tuning
trajectory on protected data aligns with an "expert" trajectory recorded from target data.

## Threat model
- Adversary objective: an attacker fine-tunes a diffusion model on the protector's (unauthorized) images to reproduce the
  subject's identity; the protector's goal is to make that fine-tuning learn a chosen *target* concept instead.
- Adversary knowledge (white/gray/black-box): protector does not have complete knowledge of the attacker's pre-trained
  model choice (author-stated real-world assumption); protection is crafted against a representative attacker (SD1.4 +
  DreamBooth+LoRA) and cross-model transfer is evaluated.
- Adversary access: attacker obtains the protected images and fine-tunes on them.
- Adversary capabilities: standard personalization fine-tuning (DreamBooth/LoRA).
- Attacker budget: not stated in paper (no bound on attacker fine-tuning compute/iterations; protector uses 3 expert
  trajectories, N=2000 noise-optimization iters, 1000 training iters).
- Phase: protective perturbation crafted pre-release; effect manifests during attacker fine-tuning (training) and shows in inference outputs.
- Targeted / untargeted: targeted protection (redirect to a specified target identity/pattern) — this is the paper's core novelty.
- Digital / physical: digital.
- Adaptive / non-adaptive: attacker is non-adaptive (no explicit adaptive/purification adversary that tries to strip the
  perturbation is evaluated — reviewer note).

## Trust assumptions
Assumes the protector can add an ℓ∞-bounded perturbation (ε = 0.05 ≈ 12.75/255) to images before they are scraped;
assumes attacker uses gradient-based personalization fine-tuning whose parameter trajectory can be steered. Verifiability
and traceability rest on the target concept/pattern appearing in generated outputs.

## Attack or failure mechanism
Unauthorized personalization: attacker fine-tunes on protected images and, absent protection, learns the true identity.
TAFAP counters by optimizing perturbation δ so the student (protected-data) weight trajectory matches a pre-recorded
target (expert) trajectory (normalized L2 distance between weight checkpoints), causing the model to instead learn the
target concept — providing identity concealing, verifiability, and traceability.

## Proposed defense or method
TAFAP (Trajectory Alignment via Fine-tuning with Adversarial Perturbations). Two steps: (1) Expert Trajectory Recording —
fine-tune on target data and store per-iteration parameters (LoRA, ~3 MB/checkpoint, 6 MB with optimizer state); (2)
Iterative Trajectory Matching — randomly select saved target weights, roll the student k steps, minimize normalized L2
distance between resulting and target weights, update δ; repeat N times. Objective normalizes by target-trajectory step
magnitude for consistent loss scaling.

## Datasets and benchmarks
Face/identity data (VGGFace2 and related face datasets referenced); Stable Diffusion 1.4/2.1 as base models; DreamBooth +
LoRA personalization. Exact dataset splits and full hyperparameters are in supplementary material (not in extracted text).

## Evaluation methodology
Compared against Mist, ACE (both UDP-oriented targeted-loss methods) and T-ASPL (first genuine TDP attempt), using only
their targeted-attack loss components for a fair TDP comparison. Evaluated across multiple noise budgets ε
(0.025–0.500). Cross-model generalization tested (SD1.4→SD2.1, with/without LoRA). Progressive identity change tracked
across noise-update iterations.

## Metrics
Identity resemblance to target vs. original (face-recognition based, e.g., ArcFace referenced), image quality
(no-reference quality metrics referenced, e.g., BRISQUE-style). Exact numeric tables are in the truncated/supplementary
portions — not fully reproduced here (per evidence-integrity rules, specific success rates are "not stated" in the
available text beyond qualitative superiority claims).

## Main findings
Author claims: TAFAP is the first method to achieve reliable targeted transformation in diffusion models with
simultaneous control over identity and low-level visual patterns; it maintains persistent (non-diluted) protection
throughout fine-tuning, outperforming Mist/ACE/T-ASPL while preserving image quality; identity transfer degree can be
modulated by the number of noise-update iterations; protection shows resilience under attacker-model mismatch.

## Negative results
Authors observe "identity blending" in certain cases (partial rather than complete redirection); they frame this
positively as controllable interpolation rather than failure. Cross-model results show "noticeable deviation from
protected identity" but "varying degrees of target resemblance" — i.e., degraded targeting under model mismatch.

## Limitations stated by the authors
Identity blending in some cases; cross-model target resemblance varies; demonstration focused on identity transformation
as a proof-of-concept for the TDP principle.

## Additional limitations identified during review
_(reviewer synthesis — not author claims)_
- No adaptive adversary: no evaluation against perturbation-purification, adversarial-training-based robustification, or
  data-cleaning defenses the attacker could deploy — protection is only shown against a non-adaptive fine-tuner.
- Requires access to protect images before scraping; provides no protection for already-public images.
- Verifiability/traceability are asserted via target-concept appearance but no formal false-positive/false-negative
  detection rate or forensic protocol is reported in the extracted text.
- Storing full per-iteration expert trajectories has memory/compute cost; scalability to many protectors/targets not analyzed.
- ε = 0.05 perturbation may be perceptible under scrutiny (authors emphasize semantic vs. perceptual modification, but no formal imperceptibility user study is in the extracted text).

## Design implications
Protective/"unlearnable-example" perturbations can be made *directional* (steer learning to a chosen concept) rather than
purely destructive; trajectory-level (not snapshot-level) optimization is the key lever for durable influence over a
downstream training process — a transferable idea for any defense that must survive an adversary's full optimization run.

## Implementation implications
Trajectory-matching protection needs to record and store the target training trajectory (LoRA keeps this cheap); protection
strength is tunable via iteration count, enabling graded protection levels.

## Evaluation implications
Protection claims should be tested across the *entire* fine-tuning trajectory and across attacker model variants, not at a
single checkpoint; snapshot-only evaluation overstates durability. Reviewer note: adaptive/purification adversaries should
be added to substantiate real-world protection.

## Deployment implications
Useful for data owners/platforms wanting verifiable, traceable safeguards on shared personal imagery; but deployment must
assume determined attackers may attempt to strip perturbations, which is untested here.

## Monitoring and incident implications
Target-concept "signature" in generated outputs offers a potential misuse-detection/traceability signal (evidence for
unauthorized-use attribution), if paired with a documented verification protocol.

## Applicability boundaries
Findings apply to diffusion-model personalization (DreamBooth/LoRA on Stable Diffusion) and image-domain data protection.
They should NOT be generalized to non-fine-tuning misuse (e.g., inference-time editing without training), to text/LLM
domains, or read as a security guarantee against adaptive attackers. This is an ML-privacy/anti-misuse method, adjacent
to (not core to) LLM-agent security.
